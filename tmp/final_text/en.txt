Abstract
Background. Cross-cohort losses are difficult to attribute when architecture and preprocessing change together.
We tested which pipeline choices remained useful before cross-cohort adaptation.
Methods. Six configurations were compared on Sleep-EDF Expanded (78 subjects) under shared 10-fold
partitions and selection rules. E1 changed only the sequence model; E2 replaced E1’s 75-dimensional C/P/N
feature-context package with a 128-dimensional current-epoch ResNet-1D encoder. Paired bootstrap and
Wilcoxon–Holm quantified uncertainty. Transfer used 180 locked SHHS1 participants without weight updates.
E6 used statistics from each complete unlabelled target recording and is therefore label-free but transductive.
Results. E3 attained the highest Sleep-EDF macro-F1 (0.7904) and exceeded E6 by 0.0213 (CI95%
[0.0122, 0.0307]; Holm 𝑝 = 0.0012); a second seed preserved the direction but not Holm significance.
Other contrasts were small and non-significant. E3 provided a 3.76× forward-pass speed-up over the CNN–
BiLSTM control, with 4.37× as many parameters and 28.4% more peak memory. On SHHS1, E3 exceeded the
control by 0.0412 subject-mean macro-F1 (CI95% [0.0314, 0.0512]) and E6 by 0.0274. E0 and E3 nevertheless
misclassified 72.3% and 73.1% of N3 epochs as N2 and had similar N3 recall (0.2610 and 0.2582); E6 reduced
recall to 0.2005.
Conclusions. The substitutions offered operational but no stable predictive gains. In this post-hoc comparison,
preprocessing was the stronger development axis. N2–N3 and N2–REM should be prioritised for labelled
adaptation; target-record normalisation alone was insufficient.

Keywords: sleep staging; single-channel EEG; temporal convolutional network; domain shift; target-domain
adaptation; paired inference.

1. Introduction
Sleep staging assigns one of five labels—W, N1, N2, N3 and REM—to every 30 s epoch of a polysomnographic
recording. Manual scoring is slow, requires trained expertise, and retains irreducible disagreement between
scorers; in the inter-scorer reliability programme of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, N1 shows the
lowest agreement of all stages [1]. An automated system therefore has to be judged not by overall accuracy

alone, which is dominated by the most frequent stages, but by class-balanced metrics, by stability across
individuals, and by its behaviour when the recording device, montage or population changes.
Progress in this field is difficult to read from published tables. Studies differ simultaneously in dataset
version, subject partitioning, wake-trimming policy, filtering, normalisation, sequence length and modelselection rule, so a reported improvement cannot be attributed to the architecture that the paper nominally
proposes. This creates a concrete development problem: before collecting target-domain labels, a practitioner
cannot tell whether a new encoder, a new sequence model or a change in signal handling is the more defensible
use of effort. A comparison in which one component changes at a time is needed to separate these decisions.
We take ZleepAnlystNet [2], a two-stage single-channel model that couples a bank of class-specific
convolutional encoders to a BiLSTM, as an internal control. E1 isolates the replacement of the BiLSTM
by a temporal convolutional network (TCN) [3]. E2 is not an isolated encoder contrast: it replaces E1’s
75-dimensional current/previous/next (C/P/N) feature-context package with a 128-dimensional current-epoch
one-dimensional residual encoder [4]. We additionally treat signal processing as an experimental factor
because filtering and record-wise normalisation can change the information available to a downstream
classifier.
The study is organised around three deployment-oriented questions. (i) Under a shared subject-wise
protocol, do the isolated TCN substitution and bundled E2 feature/context replacement provide benefits
large and stable enough to justify changing the pipeline? (ii) Which evaluated pipeline and preprocessing
choices retain their benefit under label-free cross-cohort transfer to an independent cohort with a different
EEG derivation and age distribution? (iii) When transfer performance falls, which class transitions account
for the loss and do they point to a generic preprocessing remedy or to targeted adaptation?
Our contributions are the following.
(1) A leakage-controlled 10-fold subject-wise protocol with an isolated sequence-model contrast, a clearly
labelled bundled feature/context replacement, and preprocessing contrasts under shared partitions and
model-selection rules.
(2) A locked cross-cohort evaluation on 180 SHHS Visit-1 participants, showing that the ranking and
magnitude of design choices can differ between internal validation and cross-cohort use.
(3) A class- and confusion-channel analysis showing that under-detection of N3 as N2 recurs in both the
15-CNN–BiLSTM control and ResNet-1D–TCN, while over-detection of REM from N2 is a second E3
priority. The accompanying E6 sensitivity analysis shows that per-record normalisation alone does not
resolve the cross-model N3 failure.
(4) A quantified operational trade-off: the implemented ResNet-1D–TCN pipeline reduces measured forward
latency and training-and-validation wall-clock time relative to the 15-branch CNN–BiLSTM control,
while increasing parameter count and peak memory and without establishing predictive superiority.
Throughout, we distinguish pre-specified comparisons, secondary evidence and post-hoc contrasts, and
we confine claims to the evidence class that supports them.

2. Related work
DeepSleepNet combined a dual-branch CNN with a BiLSTM to capture epoch morphology and temporal
dependence [5]. AttnSleep introduced attention over multi-resolution features [6], and SleepTransformer
applied self-attention with explicit uncertainty quantification [7]. Together these establish that sequential
context matters substantially. They also illustrate the comparability problem: differences in dataset version,
subject split and preprocessing between these works are large enough that cross-table comparison cannot
substitute for an ablation carried out inside one protocol, a point emphasised in recent reviews of the field [8].
Single-channel systems also span materially different input representations. SleepInceptionNet systematically compared raw, spectral and time–frequency inputs and selected a continuous-wavelet, central-channel
Inception model that scores one epoch at a time [9]. That study reinforces two points relevant here: a single
EEG channel can support five-stage scoring, and preprocessing choice is part of the model definition rather
than a neutral implementation detail.
Cross-dataset sleep staging is already an active methodological problem. For example, He et al. [10]

trained a domain-adaptation network across Sleep-EDF, SHHS1 and the PhysioNet Challenge data. ADAST
instead uses domain-specific attention, adversarial alignment and iterative pseudo-label self-training on
unlabelled target data [11]. These approaches adapt model representations or decision functions using the
target domain; our label-free E6 condition only estimates recording-level normalisation statistics and does
not implement domain adaptation. Van Der Donckt et al. [12] further showed that increasingly complex deep
architectures need not outperform a carefully designed conventional pipeline under a matched protocol. Our
study addresses the decision immediately before a labelled adaptation study: which existing pipeline choices
remain useful, and which class boundaries should receive limited target-domain labels?
Sleep-EDF Expanded is the standard public benchmark for single-channel EEG [13, 14]. The Sleep
Heart Health Study (SHHS) is a large multi-centre cohort assembled to study sleep-disordered breathing
and cardiovascular risk [15], distributed through the National Sleep Research Resource (NSRR) [16]. The
two differ in age, comorbidity, recording hardware, montage and label distribution. This makes SHHS a
considerably more realistic transfer test than a held-out split of the same corpus, and it is the reason we treat
the transfer result—not the in-domain ranking—as the principal evidence about deployability.
Two threads of the literature bear directly on our findings. First, the sleep slow oscillation has a pronounced
anterior amplitude maximum and propagates as a travelling wave [17], so the microvolt amplitude of
slow-wave activity is derivation-dependent; and slow-wave amplitude declines markedly with age [18]. Both
scoring manuals [19, 20] define deep sleep partly through an absolute amplitude criterion, which means an
amplitude criterion learned on one derivation and one age band need not transfer. Second, classifier outputs
trained under one label prior are miscalibrated under another, and the correction is well characterised [21, 22].
These observations motivate the class-wise transfer analysis in Section 4.5; because cohort and derivation
change together, they are treated as candidate explanations rather than mechanisms identified by this design.

3. Materials and methods
3.1. Data and labels
The Sleep Cassette subset of Sleep-EDF Expanded comprises 153 recordings from 78 subjects; we use the
Fpz–Cz derivation at 100 Hz with 30 s epochs (3,000 samples per epoch). Annotations are mapped to W, N1,
N2, N3 and REM; Movement and Unknown epochs retain their temporal position but are excluded from the
loss and from all metrics. After windowing, 195,767 epochs remain, of which 195,469 are scored and 298
are Movement/Unknown.
An evaluation window is applied identically to both cohorts. It is anchored on the first and last true sleep
epoch (any of N1, N2, N3, REM) and extended by 30 min of wake on each side, i.e. 60 epochs per side. This
policy is stated explicitly because it strongly affects the wake fraction and hence every aggregate metric, and
because it is one of the principal reasons published numbers are not directly comparable. It is a benchmark
window, not a deployable inference rule, since its anchors use ground-truth labels.
The SHHS Visit-1 sample was locked before any prediction was inspected and comprises 180 test
participants, with a further 5 adaptation and 15 validation participants that were never used to update weights.
The EEG channel is C4–A1 at 125 Hz, resampled to 100 Hz with a polyphase anti-aliasing filter (up 4, down 5,
Kaiser window 𝛽 = 5.0) applied to the continuous signal before epoching. SHHS labels are mapped 0 →W,
1 →N1, 2 →N2, {3, 4} →N3, 5 →REM, {6, 9} →excluded. Table 1 reports both label distributions.
The prior shift between cohorts is substantial: total variation distance 0.187, 𝐷 KL (EDF ∥ SHHS) = 0.101
nats. SHHS1 contains proportionally 2.02× as much N3 and 0.38× as much N1. We report the per-class
ratios in Table 1 because Section 4.5 uses their direction as evidence.

• Per-record 𝑧-score (E6): the same band-pass and clip, followed by subtraction of the mean and division

recordings and is transductive at the recording level rather than purely inductive.

the 100 Hz signal. The contrast E3 – E6 therefore evaluates a practical preprocessing choice: preserving
calibrated cross-recording amplitude relationships or applying record-wise normalisation. Because clipping
never activated in E5, E3 and E4 differ in these data by the fixed division by 100; this identity is used only to
interpret the observed contrast and does not establish equivalence or a hardware-independent scaling rule.
3.3. Model configurations
Six active configurations (E0–E4 and E6) enter performance analysis; E5 documents the cancelled clippingonly contrast. The control encoder produces 75 features from 15 CNN branches spanning current, previous
and next epochs (C/P/N). ResNet-1D produces 128 features from the current epoch only. The non-causal
six-block TCN has a 253-epoch receptive field; the BiLSTM control has 128 hidden units per direction. Both
sequence models process the full recording with masked padding. Layer dimensions and all hyperparameters

3.4. Two-stage training and subject-wise cross-validation
The 78 subjects are partitioned into 10 subject-disjoint folds. In each evaluation round one fold is the test
fold, the next fold (modulo 10) is the validation fold and the remainder are training folds. Both nights of a
subject always share the same role. The same partition is used for every configuration, and the test fold never
participates in model selection; the training API accepts only training and validation loaders, so test data
cannot enter fitting.
Training has two stages. The epoch encoder is fitted with class weights calculated from training subjects,
selected on validation data, hash-verified and used to cache features without gradients. The sequence model
is then fitted on the frozen cache using masked cross-entropy and selected by validation macro-F1. E1
reuses E0 encoder checkpoints, so E1 – E0 isolates the sequence-model substitution. In contrast, E2 – E1
changes the encoder, removes explicit P/N inputs and changes the feature dimension from 75 to 128; it is a
bundled feature/context replacement. Optimisers, learning rates, batch sizes and early-stopping settings are
in Supplementary Methods S1.
3.5. Test locking, statistical analysis and effect sizes
All six configurations completed validation-based selection before the test set was opened, once. In SleepEDF, pooled macro-F1 is the descriptive headline metric, but the unit of statistical inference is the subject.
Paired subject-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples estimates confidence intervals for the difference in
pooled macro-F1, while a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test [23] assesses the subject-level distribution of
per-subject differences [24]. Holm correction [25] is applied over the four pre-specified comparisons E1 – E0,
E2 – E1, E3 – E2 and E3 – E6. These two procedures target different estimands—a pooled, epoch-weighted
quantity and a subject-averaged rank quantity—and may therefore disagree; where they do, we say so
explicitly rather than reporting only the favourable one. E3 – E0 is labelled post-hoc throughout.
We also report win/tie/loss counts, sign dominance (𝑊 − 𝐿)/(𝑊 + 𝐿) and an unadjusted exact sign test as
descriptive robustness checks; they are not part of the pre-specified inferential family.
After the primary results had been observed, the whole campaign was repeated with a second fixed
training seed on the same partition. The two seeds are analysed separately; 𝑝-values are never pooled, and
two fixed seeds are not treated as a sample from the distribution over initialisations. This repeat is reported
as a post-protocol sensitivity analysis.
For SHHS, each configuration uses all ten Sleep-EDF outer-fold checkpoints. Softmax probabilities are
averaged in float64 across folds before the final argmax for each epoch. The pre-locked primary criterion is
subject-mean macro-F1, with E3 – E0 and E3 – E6 as the two locked comparisons under the same bootstrap
and Wilcoxon–Holm treatment. The E1/E2 component analysis is secondary evidence on an already-opened
sample, and E3 – E2 is explicitly post-hoc.
3.6. Operational evaluation
Inference timing was measured on identical hardware (Tesla V100) for all configurations and excludes
I/O, preprocessing and training. Each measurement used a batch of one synthetic 100-epoch sequence, 20
warm-up passes and three rounds of 100 timed forward passes. Configuration order was shuffled in each
round; we report the median over all 300 passes. This benchmark measures the implemented full forward
pipeline for one fixed tensor shape, not overnight wall-clock inference or algorithmic complexity.

4.3. Complexity, latency and training cost
As Table 5 shows, the ResNet-1D–TCN family provides a 3.76× measured speed-up than the CNN–BiLSTM
control while carrying 4.37× the parameters and 28.4% more peak allocated memory. The implementation
is therefore faster but not smaller. E1 differs from E0 only in the sequence model and improves latency by
just 1.07×; the larger E2–E0 difference is consequently associated primarily with replacing 15 independently
executed CNN branches by one ResNet-1D encoder, together with the full-pipeline implementation. The
benchmark does not isolate kernel-launch overhead or predict latency for other sequence lengths and hardware.

4.4. Cross-cohort evaluation on SHHS1
The seed-123 campaign preserves the direction and Holm significance of both locked SHHS contrasts,
whereas the in-domain E3 – E6 comparison no longer reaches the Holm threshold. This sensitivity result
uses the same SHHS sample and is not an independent cohort replication (Supplementary Table S6).
Both locked comparisons (Table 7) are unambiguous, and the sign-based effect sizes are large (Δsign =
+0.533 and +0.389; exact sign tests 𝑝 = 3.8 × 10−13 and 1.9 × 10−7 ). Supporting metrics move together:
relative to E0, E3 gains 0.0338 pooled macro-F1, 0.0368 accuracy and 0.0461 𝜅. Within ±1 epoch of a
transition, E3 gains 0.0366 over E0 and 0.0182 over E6.
The component analysis is more equivocal. E1 – E0 remains positive but small, supporting the TCN
substitution. E2 – E1 is negative out of domain (−0.0128, interval entirely below zero), so the bundled
replacement of E1’s 75-dimensional C/P/N package by the current-epoch 128-dimensional ResNet-1D
representation does not generalise better on unprocessed SHHS signals. This contrast cannot attribute the
reversal to the encoder alone. The post-hoc E3 – E2 contrast is the largest observed configuration difference
(+0.0475, Δsign = +0.633, 147/180 subjects improved). Because E3 and E2 share an architecture and
differ only in preprocessing, this observed post-hoc comparison identifies preprocessing as the stronger
development axis in this sample; it is not confirmatory evidence for a particular operation.
A seed-123 extension evaluating E4 (band-pass only) provides a more specific secondary contrast. E4
reached subject-mean macro-F1 0.5732, pooled macro-F1 0.6147, accuracy 0.7064, 𝜅 = 0.5869 and N1 F1
0.3492, exceeding same-seed E2 by 0.0323 and E3 by 0.0098. The E5 audit established that the ±800 𝜇V
clip never activated, so E4 and E3 differ in this dataset by the fixed division by 100. The extension therefore
associates band-pass filtering with most of the E4–E2 gain and provides no evidence that the additional
fixed scaling improves transfer. Because the SHHS sample was already open and training stochasticity is
represented by only two fixed seeds, these comparisons are secondary; they are not equivalence, non-inferiority
or universal scaling tests.

4.5. Which errors should target-domain adaptation prioritise?
E3’s pooled macro-F1 falls from 0.7904 in domain to 0.6099 out of domain, a gap of 0.1805. An aggregate
gap does not indicate whether adaptation requires broad retraining or a targeted correction. We therefore
localise the loss by class and confusion channel. This is a prioritisation analysis: it identifies where
an intervention could recover the most performance, but does not by itself identify the physiological or
acquisition mechanism that generated the errors. Throughout this analysis, A→B denotes a reference-labelled
A epoch predicted as B; it does not denote a physiological stage transition over time.
N3 is the dominant class loss: E3 N3 F1 falls from 0.8085 on Sleep-EDF to 0.4055 on SHHS1, with
precision 0.9440 but recall 0.2582. E0 shows nearly the same recall (0.2610) and N3→N2 rate (72.3% versus
73.1% for E3), so E3’s aggregate transfer advantage does not resolve N3 and the failure cannot be assigned
specifically to ResNet-1D or TCN. N2→REM is the second-largest E3 confusion channel.
The E3 oracle diagnostic ranks N3→N2 and N2→REM as potential adaptation targets, corresponding
to 74.5% and 27.5% of the numerical cross-cohort gap. These nonlinear values are neither additive nor
achievable-performance estimates. Reference-label boundary neighbourhoods are harder on both cohorts,
but low N3 recall in stable SHHS1 regions prevents a boundary-only explanation. These are post-hoc offline
diagnostics, not physiological-transition or causal analyses.
E6 provides a label-free but transductive sensitivity check. Its complete-target-record 𝑧-score lowers N3
recall to 0.2005 and leaves boundary-neighbourhood recall essentially unchanged (0.0721 versus 0.0733 for
E3). Thus, the tested normalisation is not a sufficient stand-alone remedy. Detailed class-wise, confusion,
oracle and transition results are in Supplementary Tables S7–S11.

5. Discussion
5.1. Principal findings
The first research question asked whether the sequence-model substitution and bundled E2 feature/context
replacement justified changing the pipeline. They did not establish a stable predictive advantage: both
in-domain configuration effects were small and non-significant after multiplicity correction, and the bundled
E2 – E1 contrast reversed sign on SHHS1. The supported pipeline benefit is operational: the implemented
ResNet-1D–TCN pipeline reduced measured forward latency and observed training-and-validation wall-clock
time, while increasing parameter count and peak GPU memory. It did not establish superior predictive
accuracy.
The second question asked which choices retained benefit across cohorts. The fixed-scale E3 procedure
outperformed its two locked references on SHHS1, and E3 ranked above E6 under both seeds. Under a
fixed ResNet-1D–TCN architecture, the post-hoc E3–E2 contrast was larger than the E1–E0 sequence-model
contrast and the bundled E2–E1 feature/context contrast; the seed-123 E4 extension and the E5 identity audit

The third question asked where adaptation should focus. N3→N2 recurs at essentially the same rate
in E0 and E3, while the E3 oracle analysis ranks it above N2→REM in potential leverage; the N3 deficit
becomes extreme near reference-label changes but remains substantial in stable N3. The evidence therefore
identifies a target-domain failure shared across the evaluated pipelines, not an E3-specific weakness or
a boundary-only phenomenon. Record-wise 𝑧-scoring does not repair N3, so a labelled, class-specific
target-domain adaptation experiment is better motivated than another generic architecture or normalisation
change.
5.2. From error localisation to an adaptation decision
The high precision and low recall of N3, the concentration of N3 errors in N2, and the further recall loss near
stage transitions are consistent with a conservative N3 decision boundary in SHHS1. E0’s near-identical N3
recall and N3→N2 rate show that this failure persists across the two model families evaluated; they do not
identify a shared causal mechanism. Montage and age are plausible contributors: the model was trained
on Fpz–Cz in a younger cohort and evaluated on C4–A1 in an older cohort, while slow-wave amplitude
varies with both derivation and age [17, 18]. However, montage, population, acquisition and scoring practice
change together between the datasets. The present design cannot assign the failure to one of them, and the
precision–recall pattern alone does not identify an amplitude threshold as the mechanism.
The difference in class prevalence is also relevant. A prior-shift correction would move N3 and N1 in the
required directions, but it was not fitted or validated here. It should therefore be evaluated as a low-cost
baseline in the next target-domain experiment, alongside class-specific calibration and limited fine-tuning;
the present error pattern does not justify dismissing it or treating it as a complete solution.
E6 provides an important boundary on the interpretation. If removal of record-level absolute scale were a
sufficient correction, E6 should improve N3 recall over E3. Instead, E6 lowers whole-sample N3 recall from
0.2582 to 0.2005 and leaves transition-region recall essentially unchanged (0.0721 versus 0.0733). Thus, the
data do not support absolute amplitude scale as a sufficient explanation, and record-wise 𝑧-scoring is not a
useful stand-alone adaptation strategy for this failure.
The actionable result is narrower but better supported. Before deployment on SHHS-like data, the next
experiment should target the N2–N3 decision boundary using labelled target-domain examples and should
report N3 recall specifically near transitions. A small calibration or fine-tuning study on the held-out
SHHS adaptation participants would test whether the boundary can be moved without increasing false N3
predictions. The N2–REM channel should be the second target. This prioritisation follows directly from
the error counts; the choice between montage-aware features, class-specific calibration and target-domain
fine-tuning remains an open experimental question.
5.3. Architecture and preprocessing implications
E1 – E0 isolates the TCN substitution; E2 – E1 jointly changes the encoder, explicit P/N context and
feature dimension and cannot establish a ResNet-only effect. Neither in-domain contrast survives Holm
correction, and the bundled E2 – E1 contrast reverses on SHHS1. The reliable advantage of the implemented
ResNet-1D–TCN pipeline is therefore operational: a 3.76× benchmark speed-up and 10.2–12.2× observed
training-and-validation speed-up, paid for with more parameters and memory. In the observed post-hoc
E3 – E2 comparison, preprocessing is the stronger development axis. The E4 extension associates band-pass
filtering with much of that difference, but the opened cohort and two fixed seeds preclude a causal or universal
claim about any operation.
5.4. Positioning relative to published results
We do not claim state-of-the-art benchmark performance. The wake-trimming policy alone (Section 3.1)
moves the wake fraction, and hence accuracy and macro-F1, by an amount comparable to every architectural
effect measured here; subject-count, split scheme, and channel choice compound this. Published Sleep-EDF

of these dimensions simultaneously, so a numerical comparison would misrepresent the evidence in either
direction. Our E0 control is a re-implementation of the ZleepAnlystNet design evaluated under our protocol,
and it is the reference for the component questions posed here. The study should therefore be read as evidence
about relative design choices and transfer failure under a shared protocol, not as a leaderboard claim.
5.5. Limitations and a prioritised agenda
Results should be read within the data and protocol evaluated. SHHS1 is one specific out-of-domain
sample, not a population. The second seed corroborates direction but two fixed seeds cannot characterise
initialisation variance; a proper treatment would require enough seeds to estimate a distribution, and we
report no such claim. Several component and reference-transition analyses were run after the sample was
opened and are supporting evidence only. Single-channel EEG limits separability for N1 and REM in
principle, and the transition neighbourhood uses reference labels on both sides of a boundary, so it is an
offline benchmarking device rather than a deployable rule. No probability-calibration or selective- prediction
analysis was performed, which matters for clinical use where an abstain option would be valuable [22, 7].
Two reporting gaps remain. Absolute-performance intervals are available for the SHHS configurations
(Table 6) but not for the Sleep-EDF ones, where only difference intervals were retained. And although
paired win/tie/loss counts and median subject-level differences were preserved for every comparison, the full
per-subject macro-F1 distributions were not archived, so we cannot report the dispersion (IQR, range) of
individual performance or identify which participants the models handle worst. For a metric whose clinical
relevance is inherently per-patient this is a limitation. Per-class SHHS metrics have now been recovered
for E6 from the locked prediction artifacts. Pooled class-wise results for E0 have also been recovered
from the locked run manifest and support the cross-model N3 comparison; transition-neighbourhood and
stable-interior metrics are available for E0 and E3 on both cohorts. Corresponding class-wise results for E1
remain unavailable.
Ordered by expected practical value, we would prioritise: (1) limited class-specific fine-tuning or decisionboundary adjustment on the held-out SHHS adaptation participants, evaluated on N3 recall and false-positive
rate in both boundary and stable regions; (2) fitting and evaluating calibration/selective prediction on
independent target validation data; and (3) estimating initialisation variance with a sufficient number of
seeds. The first two address the deployment failure identified here; the third improves the strength of the
methodological evidence.

6. Conclusion
A shared subject-wise protocol allowed six active configurations to be compared on the same Sleep-EDF
folds; a seventh planned configuration, E5, was excluded before performance analysis because it was bitwise
identical to E4. The isolated TCN substitution and bundled E2 feature/context replacement did not establish
a stable predictive advantage after correction for multiple comparisons. ResNet-1D–TCN nevertheless offers
an operational alternative that is 3.76× faster in the controlled forward benchmark and 10.2–12.2× faster in
observed training-and-validation wall-clock time, at the cost of more parameters and memory.
On SHHS1, the fixed-scale E3 procedure outperformed both locked references, while the largest observed
component difference was associated with preprocessing under a fixed architecture. This result shifts the
practical priority from further architecture replacement to signal handling and target-domain evaluation,
without establishing any individual preprocessing operation as causal.
Most importantly, the aggregate transfer loss resolves into concrete adaptation targets. E0 and E3 both
systematically under-call N3 as N2, showing that E3’s overall transfer advantage does not solve N3 detection;
N2 is also frequently over-called as REM by E3. Per-record 𝑧-scoring does not repair the N3 deficit, and the
cross-cohort diagnostic shows that reference-label boundaries intensify errors without fully explaining the
stable-region SHHS1 deficit. Thus, neither the evaluated architecture changes nor generic normalisation is a
sufficient deployment remedy. The evidence supports a targeted, labelled adaptation study on these decision
boundaries; it does not yet support an architecture-specific, physiological or clinical claim.

retained in the project repository. Sleep-EDF Expanded is publicly available from PhysioNet. SHHS

used for the reported analyses were hash-verified against their run manifests. The E6 class-wise results are therefore reported as a descriptive reanalysis of these locked predictions. The historical
locked-protocol snapshot configs/shhs_zero_shot_v1.json is retained in the repository and its SHA256 matches the protocol hash recorded in the SHHS run manifest (‘165d7cdf...fe93‘). The newer
configs/shhs_v1_protocol.json has a different SHA-256 (‘9541e233...fe9‘) because it is an expanded
post-run audit record; it must not be substituted for the historical snapshot when interpreting the locked
predictions. Protocol provenance is therefore traceable, although the locked E6 results remain a descriptive
reanalysis rather than a rerun.
