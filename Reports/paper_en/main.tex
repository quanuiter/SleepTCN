\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Background.} Model and preprocessing choices can alter sleep-staging accuracy,
computational cost and cross-cohort errors.

\noindent
\textbf{Methods.} Six configurations were compared on 78 Sleep-EDF subjects using shared 10-fold
partitions, paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon--Holm analysis. Primary transfer used 180 Sleep Heart Health
Study Visit 1 (SHHS1) participants without weight updates. Record-wise normalisation was transductive.
Matched interventions included ADAST at limited and full-source budgets.

\noindent
\textbf{Results.} The fixed-scale one-dimensional residual network (ResNet-1D) with temporal
convolutional network (TCN) configuration
(E3) reached pooled Sleep-EDF \mf\ 0.7904, exceeding its record-wise-normalised counterpart (E6) by
0.0213 (\ci\ $[0.0122,0.0307]$; Holm $p=0.0012$). A second seed preserved the direction but not
Holm significance. E3 delivered a $3.76\times$ forward-pass speed-up over the convolutional--bidirectional
long short-term memory (CNN--BiLSTM) control,
with $4.37\times$ as many parameters. On SHHS1, it improved subject-mean \mf\ over the control by
0.0412 (\ci\ $[0.0314,0.0512]$). Additional same-cohort comparisons favoured band-pass-only E4
over E3 at both seeds: differences of 0.0131 (seed 42, \ci\ $[0.0111,0.0153]$)
and 0.0098 (seed 123, \ci\ $[0.0073,0.0125]$).
Both primary pipelines misclassified over 72\% of N3 (deep-sleep) epochs as N2;
E3 N3 recall was 0.2582. Full-source ADAST improved subject-mean \mf\ by 0.0445
(\ci\ $[0.0352,0.0539]$) over its matched control; N3 recall increased
0.2432 to 0.6111, with lower N1/N2/REM F1.

\noindent
\textbf{Conclusions.} E3 combined faster computation with better aggregate transfer performance than
the control; band-pass-only E4 achieved higher SHHS1 scores. N3-to-N2 confusion dominated deep-sleep
errors. These findings support joint evaluation of model design, preprocessing and stage-specific performance.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sleep staging; single-channel EEG; temporal convolutional network;
preprocessing; domain shift; paired evaluation.

\section{Introduction}

Sleep staging assigns one of five labels---W, N1, N2, N3 and REM---to every 30\,s epoch of a
polysomnographic recording. Manual scoring is slow, requires trained expertise, and retains
disagreement between scorers; in the inter-scorer reliability programme of the American
Academy of Sleep Medicine, N1 shows the lowest agreement of all stages~\cite{rosenberg2013aasm}. An
automated system therefore has to be judged not by overall accuracy alone, which is dominated by the
most frequent stages, but by class-balanced metrics, by stability across individuals, and by its
behaviour when the recording device, montage or population changes.

Progress in this field is difficult to read from published tables. Studies differ simultaneously in
dataset version, subject partitioning, wake-trimming policy, filtering, normalisation, sequence length
and model-selection rule, making architectural effects difficult to distinguish from other pipeline
differences across studies. For practical development, the relevant questions are which pipeline choices
improve prediction, what they cost computationally, and whether their benefits persist on another cohort.
Answering these questions requires shared evaluation partitions and an explicit account of what changes
between configurations, including their training procedures.

We take ZleepAnlystNet~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}, a two-stage single-channel model that couples a
bank of class-specific convolutional encoders to a BiLSTM, as an internal control. E1 replaces the
BiLSTM configuration with a temporal convolutional network (TCN)~\cite{bai2018tcn}, reusing the encoder
and cached features but changing the sequence-model training recipe. E2 replaces E1's 75-dimensional
current/previous/next (C/P/N) feature-context package with a 128-dimensional current-epoch residual
encoder~\cite{he2016resnet}, also changing encoder training and selection. These are comparisons of
specified configurations, not isolated architectural effects. Preprocessing contrasts then examine
filtering and amplitude handling within the same ResNet-1D--TCN model and training configuration.

Model and signal-processing choices can change both prediction quality and computational cost,
but good performance on one dataset may not carry over to another. We therefore ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item How do the evaluated model configurations differ in their ability to classify sleep stages
and in the computational resources they require?
\item With the model architecture and training settings held fixed, how do filtering and amplitude
normalisation choices affect classification performance?
\item When applied to data from a different group of people without updating model weights,
which configurations perform better, and which sleep stages remain difficult to recognise?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item We quantify the trade-offs between classification performance and computational cost across
the evaluated configurations. The implemented ResNet-1D--TCN pipeline requires less computation time
than the CNN--BiLSTM control, despite having more parameters.
\item We show that preprocessing choices can substantially affect performance on another dataset.
Even with the ResNet-1D--TCN architecture and training settings held fixed, filtering and amplitude
handling lead to different results on SHHS1. These comparisons show why preprocessing needs to be
evaluated alongside the model.
\item We identify N3-to-N2 confusion as a shared cross-cohort error: both CNN--BiLSTM and
ResNet-1D--TCN misclassify more than 72\% of reference N3 epochs as N2. Matched intervention
experiments quantify how class weighting and domain adaptation increase N3 recall while changing
recognition of the other stages.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

DeepSleepNet combined a dual-branch CNN with a BiLSTM to capture epoch morphology and temporal
dependence~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet}. AttnSleep introduced attention over multi-resolution
features~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep}, and SleepTransformer applied self-attention with explicit
uncertainty quantification~\cite{phan2022sleeptransformer}. Together these establish that sequential
context matters substantially. They also illustrate the comparability problem: differences in dataset
version, subject split and preprocessing between these works are large enough that cross-table
comparison cannot substitute for an ablation carried out inside one protocol, a point emphasised in
recent reviews of the field~\cite{phan2022review}.

Single-channel systems also span materially different input representations. SleepInceptionNet
systematically compared raw, spectral and time--frequency inputs and selected a continuous-wavelet,
central-channel Inception model that scores one epoch at a time~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet}.
That study reinforces two points relevant here: a single EEG channel can support five-stage scoring, and
preprocessing choice is part of the model definition rather than a neutral implementation detail.

Cross-dataset sleep staging is already an active methodological problem. For example, He et
al.~\cite{he2023crossscenario} trained a domain-adaptation network across Sleep-EDF, SHHS1 and the
PhysioNet Challenge data. ADAST instead uses domain-specific attention, adversarial alignment and
iterative pseudo-label self-training on unlabelled target data~\cite{eldele2023adast}. These approaches
adapt model representations or decision functions using the target domain; our label-free E6 condition
only estimates recording-level normalisation statistics, with no learned target-domain or weight adaptation.
Van Der Donckt et al.~\cite{vdd2023traditional} further showed that increasingly complex deep
architectures need not outperform a carefully designed conventional pipeline under a matched protocol.
Our contribution is a joint assessment of pipeline substitutions, preprocessing, computational cost
and cross-cohort error structure. The paired comparisons quantify benefits within a common protocol;
the cross-model analysis tests whether an aggregate transfer gain also addresses the dominant stage-specific
failure. Neither question is answered by comparing scores from unrelated benchmark tables.

Sleep-EDF Expanded is the standard public benchmark for single-channel
EEG~\cite{kemp2000sleepEDF,physionetSleepEDF,pollard2026physionet}. The Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS) is a large
multi-centre cohort assembled to study sleep-disordered breathing and cardiovascular
risk~\cite{quan1997shhs}, distributed through the National Sleep Research Resource
(NSRR)~\cite{zhang2018nsrr}. The two differ in age, comorbidity, recording hardware, montage and label
distribution. SHHS therefore tests a form of cohort change not represented by a held-out split of
Sleep-EDF. We use it to assess cross-cohort robustness, without equating retrospective transfer testing
with clinical deployment validation.

Two further strands of work inform the interpretation. Slow oscillations have a spatially varying
amplitude and propagate across the cortex~\cite{massimini2004travelling}, while deep-sleep scoring uses
amplitude criteria~\cite{rechtschaffen1968,berry2017aasm}. A recent SHHS analysis examined how those
criteria relate to age- and sex-associated differences in N3 scoring~\cite{davidson2025n3}. Such work
provides context for transfer between derivations and populations, rather than a mechanism established
by the present experiment. Differences in class prevalence can also affect predictions; prior correction
and probability calibration address related but distinct problems~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,guo2017calibration}.
We therefore examine class-wise errors and label proportions without treating marginal prediction
counts as a calibration measure.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data and labels}

Our Sleep Cassette analysis sample from Sleep-EDF Expanded comprises 153 recordings from 78 subjects; we use the
Fpz--Cz derivation at 100\,Hz with 30\,s epochs (3{,}000 samples per epoch). Annotations are mapped to
W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, merging the original stages 3 and 4 into N3; Movement and Unknown epochs retain their temporal position but are excluded from
the loss and from all metrics. After windowing, 195{,}767 epochs remain, of which 195{,}469 are scored
and 298 are Movement/Unknown.

An evaluation window is applied identically to both cohorts. It is anchored on the first and last true
sleep epoch (any of N1, N2, N3, REM) and extended by 30\,min of wake on each side, i.e.\ 60 epochs per
side. This policy is stated explicitly because it strongly affects the wake fraction and hence every
aggregate metric, and because it is one of the principal reasons published numbers are not directly
comparable. It is a benchmark window, not a deployable inference rule, since its anchors use ground-truth
labels.

The SHHS Visit-1 selection used the metadata quality field \texttt{overall\_shhs1}, admitting values
3--7. Eligible participant identifiers were ranked deterministically by SHA-256 with selection seed 42,
then independently ranked for role assignment: 5 adaptation, 15 validation, 180 test and 20 reserve
participants. These are the archived protocol's role names; in the primary experiments the adaptation
group served technical checks, not model adaptation. No reserve replacements were required. The
180-person test sample was selected before prediction review. Neither the adaptation nor validation
group updated weights in the primary experiments; the additional ADAST experiment uses only the
five adaptation participants' unlabelled signals for weight updates.
The EEG channel is C4--A1 at 125\,Hz, resampled to 100\,Hz with a polyphase anti-aliasing
filter (up 4, down 5, Kaiser window $\beta = 5.0$) applied to the continuous signal before epoching.
SHHS labels are mapped $0\rightarrow$W, $1\rightarrow$N1, $2\rightarrow$N2, $\{3,4\}\rightarrow$N3,
$5\rightarrow$REM, $\{6,9\}\rightarrow$excluded. Table~\ref{tab:class-distribution} reports both label
distributions.

\subsection{Preprocessing conditions}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the evaluated architecture, context and preprocessing
configurations and their shared evaluation protocol. Calibrated EEG is processed continuously
before epoching; the resulting epochs are encoded by either the 15-CNN or ResNet-1D encoder,
then modelled as a sequence by a BiLSTM or TCN.

All conditions operate on the continuous calibrated signal in microvolts before epoching. Four active
signal conditions enter the performance analysis, and one audit-only condition (E5) records a planned
clipping ablation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Raw} (E0--E2): no filtering, no clipping, no rescaling; native $\mu$V retained.
\item \textbf{Band-pass only} (E4): zero-phase Butterworth band-pass, order 4, 0.5--30\,Hz, implemented
as second-order sections with forward--backward filtering.
\item \textbf{Band-pass plus clipping} (E5; audit only): the E4 signal followed by a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V. Across all 153 Sleep-EDF recordings, E5 and E4 were bitwise identical and the measured
clipped-sample fraction was zero. Fold 0 was retained as an audit artifact; the remaining folds and all
performance tests were cancelled before scores were inspected because E5 did not define an independent
input condition.
\item \textbf{Fixed-scale amplitude handling} (E3): the same band-pass, followed by a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V and division by a constant factor of 100. The clip threshold is an absolute constant
identical for every recording, so no record-level or cohort-level statistic is estimated and
cross-subject leakage is not introduced; relative amplitude between recordings is preserved.
\item \textbf{Per-record $z$-score} (E6): the same band-pass and clip, followed by subtraction of the
mean and division by the standard deviation computed over the whole filtered, clipped recording. This
uses no target labels but does use all samples of the target recording, removes absolute amplitude
differences between recordings and is transductive at the recording level rather than purely inductive.
\end{itemize}

No notch filter is applied: 50\,Hz lies outside the 0.5--30\,Hz pass-band and at the Nyquist frequency
of the 100\,Hz signal. The contrast E3\,--\,E6 therefore evaluates a practical preprocessing choice:
preserving calibrated cross-recording amplitude relationships or applying record-wise normalisation.
Because clipping never activated in the Sleep-EDF E5 audit, E3 and E4 differ on Sleep-EDF by the fixed division by 100;
the corresponding SHHS audit also found zero clipped samples. The E3--E4 comparison therefore tests
training and evaluation at two input scales on these data, not an active clipping effect.

Six active configurations (E0--E4 and E6) enter performance analysis; E5 documents the cancelled
clipping-only contrast. The control encoder concatenates five-class softmax outputs from 15 CNN branches
spanning current, previous and next epochs (C/P/N), giving 75 features per central epoch.
Context stays within each recording, with edge-epoch replication. ResNet-1D produces 128 features from the current epoch only. The
non-causal six-block TCN has a 253-epoch receptive field; the BiLSTM control has 128 hidden units per
direction. Both sequence models process the retained recording with masked padding. These are offline
models: the BiLSTM and TCN use future context, and zero-phase filtering and E6 normalisation use future
signal samples. The forward benchmark is not a measure of online decision latency. Layer dimensions and
all hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Two-stage training and subject-wise cross-validation}

The 78 subjects are partitioned into 10 subject-disjoint folds. In each evaluation round one fold is the
test fold, the next fold (modulo 10) is the validation fold and the remainder are training folds. Both
nights of a subject always share the same role. The same partition is used for every configuration, and
the test fold never participates in model selection. The training API accepts only training and
validation loaders, and encoder fitting and feature caching follow the same subject-fold assignments.

Training has two stages. The epoch encoder uses class weights calculated from training subjects and
is selected on validation data: minimum weighted validation loss for each control CNN, maximum
validation \mf\ for ResNet. Its selected checkpoint is hash-verified and used to cache features
without gradients. The sequence model is fitted on the frozen cache using masked cross-entropy and
selected by validation \mf. E1 reuses E0 encoder checkpoints but changes the sequence optimiser
settings: learning rate 0.01 versus 0.0005, batch size 4 versus 8 recordings, maximum 1{,}000 versus
300 epochs and patience 10 versus 30 for E0 and E1, respectively. E2 additionally changes encoder
optimisation and selection alongside the feature/context replacement. Supplementary Methods S1
provides the complete recipes. The comparisons therefore preserve subject partitions and evaluation,
not every training choice.

\subsection{Statistical analysis and evaluation protocol}

All six configurations completed validation-based selection before the primary seed-42 test predictions
were evaluated. In
Sleep-EDF, pooled \mf\ is the descriptive headline metric, but the unit of statistical inference is the
subject. Paired subject-cluster bootstrap with 10{,}000 resamples estimates percentile confidence intervals for the
difference in pooled \mf~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}, while a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} assesses the
subject-level distribution of per-subject differences. Holm
correction~\cite{holm1979} is applied over the four pre-specified comparisons E1\,--\,E0, E2\,--\,E1,
E3\,--\,E2 and E3\,--\,E6. Pooled \mf\ is computed from the sum of subject confusion matrices;
subject-mean \mf\ is the arithmetic mean of individual subjects' scores. They are not interchangeable,
and pooled \mf\ is not a weighted average of individual \mf\ values. The bootstrap interval describes
the pooled-score difference, whereas Wilcoxon evaluates the paired subject differences through their
signed ranks. All bootstrap intervals are marginal 95\% intervals, not multiplicity-adjusted simultaneous
intervals. Supplementary Methods S1 specifies bootstrap seeds and zero-difference handling.
Metrics use five fixed classes and assign zero when a class-wise denominator is zero.
In the Sleep-EDF analysis, E3\,--\,E0 is labelled post-hoc throughout.

We also report win/tie/loss counts, sign dominance $(W-L)/(W+L)$ and an unadjusted exact sign test as
descriptive robustness checks; they are not part of the pre-specified inferential family.

The completed seed-123 campaign repeated the evaluation with a second fixed training seed on the
same partition. The two seeds are analysed separately; $p$-values are never pooled,
and this repeat provides a sensitivity check across the two initialisations. We report it
separately from the primary campaign. The archived configuration lists
seeds 42, 123 and 2025; only completed campaigns 42 and 123 contribute results here.

For SHHS, each configuration uses all ten Sleep-EDF outer-fold checkpoints. Softmax probabilities are
averaged in float64 across folds before the final argmax for each epoch. The prespecified primary criterion
is subject-mean \mf, with E3\,--\,E0 and E3\,--\,E6 as the two primary comparisons under the same
subject-cluster bootstrap and Wilcoxon--Holm treatment, now with bootstrap intervals for the
subject-mean difference. The E1/E2 analysis is secondary evidence on an already-opened sample,
and E3\,--\,E2 is explicitly post-hoc. The seed-123 E4 extension has its own four-comparison Holm
family, reported in the supplement. A subsequent E4 seed-42 comparison used the restored historical
source-selected checkpoints and identical benchmark windows; all ten folds were averaged without
target-based ranking. Its paired bootstrap intervals are reported separately, without adding a
new $p$-value to the primary family. Reference-label boundary and stable-region analyses are post-hoc
offline diagnostics; their exact masks, exclusions and supports are defined in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Additional intervention experiments}

After characterising the SHHS1 errors, we evaluated temperature scaling with EM prior correction,
class-weighted TCN training, and a harmonised ADAST comparison~\cite{eldele2023adast}. These
follow-up experiments use the same 180 SHHS1 participants. Calibration uses all
ten seed-123 E3 folds: each temperature is fitted on source validation, and each EM prior on five
fixed unlabelled adaptation participants, disjoint from test. Class weighting uses ten matched
seed-123 pairs, with the corresponding historical E3 encoder frozen in each fold and a newly
trained unweighted control. Both arms average all ten fold probabilities. Training used CPU
for folds 0--5 and CUDA for folds 6--9; these timings are excluded from the historical operational
benchmark. The earlier fold-0 result is retained separately. ADAST uses ten freshly trained matched
CUDA pairs, seed 123, with a source-only dual-head control in each fold. Both arms use 1,140 updates
per fold and average all ten fold probabilities; source outer-test results are out-of-fold predictions,
not ensembles. The earlier CPU single-fold ADAST pilot is retained separately, not pooled into the
CUDA experiment. All arms infer complete target recordings before the same
historical scoring mask is applied; calibration therefore uses a rerun full-record E3 control.
Model selection uses source validation, and adaptation uses unlabelled target data; target test
labels are used for scoring. Supplementary Methods S2 specifies the fitting, selection and adaptation budgets. Paired
participant-bootstrap intervals for these experiments are reported separately from the primary tests.

Subsequent development experiments on source fold 0 (seed 123) compared 1,140 with 36,870
updates per model, followed by a fresh full-source ADAST reference and three single-factor loss
ablations. All models ran for 30 epochs on T4 CUDA. Checkpoints were selected by maximum
source-attention source-validation \mf, retaining the first tie; epoch-30 checkpoints were
reported separately. The five unlabelled adaptation participants were reused, without source
outer-test or SHHS test access. Both attention paths were evaluated on the same source validation
set. These development comparisons are separate from the ten-fold transfer evaluation
(Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_budget_development} and~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_loss_development}).

A subsequent three-arm check used source fold 1 with the same seed, comparing fresh
source-only, ADAST-reference and retained-source-loss models. Each model received 35,820
updates over 30 epochs, covering all 152,825 training epochs per pass. Selection and
adaptation data were unchanged in role; the source-validation set contained 23,881 epochs.
Both attention paths were evaluated at the selected and epoch-30 checkpoints
(Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_confirmation} and~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_confirmation_attention}).

To test transfer-budget sensitivity, we completed ten matched full-source pairs at seed 123:
16 new models on source folds 2--9 and four compatible models reused from folds 0--1,
excluding the loss-intervention arms. Each model received 30 complete source-training passes,
35,670--37,620 updates depending on fold, with identical initialisation and source order within
each pair. ADAST reused the same five unlabelled adaptation participants. Full-source training
also increases target exposure and changes scheduler timing in update units; it is a learning-budget
comparison, not an isolated source-coverage intervention. The primary comparison uses epoch 30
in both arms at both budgets. Maximum dual-head logits are softmaxed per fold and probabilities
are averaged across ten folds; SHHS uses source attention for source-only and target attention
for ADAST. Sleep-EDF outer-test predictions use the corresponding fold and source attention in
both arms. We estimate ADAST minus source-only at each budget and the difference between these
effects, using the same 10,000 participant-bootstrap draws across all four systems. These
intervals condition on the fitted models and do not include training variability
(Table~\ref{tab:adast-budget-main}; Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_fullsource_classes}).
For the secondary full-source evaluation, each model uses the checkpoint with maximum
source-validation \mf\ through source attention, retaining the first epoch on a tie.
Attention paths, aggregation and scoring are unchanged; SHHS labels do not enter selection.

\subsection{Operational evaluation}

Inference timing was measured on identical hardware (Tesla V100) for all configurations and excludes

each round; we report the median over all 300 passes. This benchmark measures the implemented full
forward pipeline for one fixed tensor shape, not overnight wall-clock inference or algorithmic

again increased selected N1 F1 (0.2175 to 0.2302), but reduced REM F1 (0.6852 to 0.6680).
N3 F1 increased from 0.8096 to 0.8161 through source attention, whereas it decreased from
0.8003 to 0.7864 through target attention on the same source validation set. Thus, the
selected-checkpoint N1 benefit recurred alongside lower REM F1 and aggregate \mf.
At epoch 30, N1 F1 was lower with retained source loss (0.2158 versus 0.2175), showing that
the selected-checkpoint improvement did not persist to the final epoch
(Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_confirmation} and~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_confirmation_attention}).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the pipeline comparisons establish}

The primary comparison shows operational and cross-cohort gains for E3 relative to
the CNN--BiLSTM control. ResNet-1D--TCN
processes the benchmark input $3.76\times$ faster than the CNN--BiLSTM control,
despite having more parameters. Its observed training-and-validation time is also shorter,
although the recipes and stopping rules differ. On the SHHS1 sample, E3 improves subject-mean
\mf\ over E0 by 0.0412, with a positive interval and gains in 138 of 180 participants. These results
support E3 as a faster, higher-scoring alternative to that control in this setting.
For the preprocessing comparison, E4 attained the higher SHHS1 subject-mean score in both seeds.

The intermediate comparisons explain why that benefit should not be credited simply to a newer
encoder or sequence model. E1--E0 changes both the sequence model and its training recipe;
E2--E1 changes the encoder, feature context and encoder training. Neither contrast reaches the
subject-level Holm threshold on Sleep-EDF, and E2--E1 reverses direction on SHHS1. By contrast,
the observed post-hoc E3--E2 preprocessing difference is larger on SHHS1. The seed-123 extension
further shows that E4, which retains band-pass filtering without fixed scaling, outperforms both E2
and E3 on that sample. Signal handling is therefore a material part of the evaluated model's transfer
behaviour, rather than a negligible implementation detail. These configuration-level comparisons do
not isolate the contribution of each preprocessing operation in E3--E2.

The E3--E4 contrast is narrower because clipping was inactive in both cohorts. Multiplication by
a positive constant preserves waveform shape and relative spectral power; it does not selectively
remove slow waves. The comparison instead concerns separately trained models at two input scales.
E4's advantage therefore concerns training and evaluating the model without division by 100,
rather than recovering slow-wave information removed by that operation. Separating effects on
optimisation from effects on learned normalisation statistics would require additional controlled
training comparisons.

\subsection{N3 under-detection persists alongside aggregate improvement}

The class-wise analysis reveals a shared failure mode alongside E3's overall advantage. E0 and E3 both
misclassify more than 72\% of true N3 as N2, with recall near 0.26 despite precision above 0.90.
The alternative E6 pipeline has still lower N3 recall. This common failure persists despite the better
aggregate score. The oracle calculation shows the numerical importance of
N3$\rightarrow$N2 for E3, while the regional analysis shows that the deficit is present in stable
N3 as well as around reference-label changes. Together, these observations identify a more specific
cross-model error pattern than the overall transfer score alone.

The cohorts differ simultaneously in EEG derivation, population, acquisition and scoring conditions.
Slow-wave spatial structure and amplitude-based N3 scoring criteria motivate examining these
differences~\cite{massimini2004travelling,davidson2025n3}. The present design measures their combined
effect rather than separating individual causes. E6 evaluates record-wise $z$-scoring during both
source training and target inference; its lower N3 recall shows that this particular normalisation
pipeline further reduces N3 detection.

The intervention experiments reveal different responses to attempts to increase N3 detection.
EM converged to priors near zero for N3 and eliminated N3 predictions. Class weighting increased
N3 recall and F1 but reduced N2 recall and accuracy, with a subject-mean \mf\ difference of
$-0.0016$ (\ci\ $[-0.0049,0.0017]$). Limited-budget ADAST increased N3 recall but reduced
aggregate \mf\ and eliminated target N1 predictions. With matched full-source training,
ADAST instead improved target subject-mean \mf\ by 0.0445, with a 0.3679 increase in N3
recall; N1, N2 and REM F1 remained lower than their matched controls. The sign change in
the adaptation effect shows why training budget matters when interpreting a UDA baseline.
Greater REM recall did not imply better REM F1 because precision fell. Thus, an increase
in N3 recall should be assessed together with
precision, other-stage errors and aggregate performance. N2$\rightarrow$REM provides a second
important error channel for E3.

\subsection{Positioning and limits of generalisation}

This study connects paired pipeline comparisons with computational trade-offs and cross-cohort error
structure, complementing work on preprocessing, input representations and conventional/deep pipeline
comparisons~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet,vdd2023traditional}. E0 is a reimplementation of the
ZleepAnlystNet design under the stated protocol. The primary comparisons evaluate transfer
without weight updates; the additional ADAST experiment evaluates target-domain weight updates
against a matched source-only control.

The study evaluates transfer from Sleep-EDF to SHHS1, with two training seeds for the principal
configuration comparisons. Follow-up interventions reuse the same SHHS1 test cohort; adaptation
uses five separate unlabelled participants. Sleep-EDF uses out-of-fold predictions and SHHS uses
ten-model ensembles, so their score difference combines cohort and evaluation-procedure differences.
The models use future context and were evaluated offline; forward-pass timing therefore describes
offline processing rather than real-time deployment. Class-wise results are pooled across participants,
without sex- or gender-stratified analysis. Further evaluation across cohorts, montages and subgroups
would test the consistency of the observed N3 pattern and intervention trade-offs.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

The evaluated ResNet-1D--TCN pipeline combined lower computational time with improved aggregate
performance relative to the CNN--BiLSTM control. E3 achieved pooled Sleep-EDF \mf\ 0.7904 and
improved subject-mean \mf\ on the 180-person SHHS1 sample by 0.0412 over E0, with Holm-adjusted
significance. Its forward pass was $3.76\times$ faster in the fixed benchmark, and its seed-123
training-and-validation time was $10.2\times$ shorter, despite the larger parameter count.
These are benefits of the evaluated configurations, including their training recipes.

Preprocessing materially affected the observed cross-cohort ranking. The seed-42 E3--E2 post-hoc
contrast favoured the combined filtering/clip/fixed-scale pipeline over raw inputs, whereas additional
comparisons at seeds 42 and 123 favoured band-pass-only E4 over same-seed E3.
E4 is therefore the stronger preprocessing option for SHHS1 among these same-seed comparisons.
The results support evaluating signal processing jointly with model design.

E0 and E3 both classified more
than 72\% of SHHS1 N3 epochs as N2, and N3 under-detection persisted in the tested record-wise
normalisation configuration. EM prior correction did not remedy this deficit. Additional
ten-fold class-weighted training improved N3 recall at the cost of
N2 recall and accuracy, with a subject-mean \mf\ difference interval spanning zero. Ten-fold ADAST's
effect depended on learning budget: the limited-budget comparison reduced class-balanced
performance and eliminated N1 predictions, whereas full-source training increased subject-mean
\mf\ by 0.0445 and N3 recall from 0.2432 to 0.6111 relative to its matched source-only control.
The full-source benefit retained costs in N1, N2 and REM F1 and source outer-test performance.
Together, these experiments identify N3-to-N2 confusion as a principal transfer error and quantify
the gains and costs of three intervention strategies. Model and preprocessing selection should
therefore combine computational measurements with aggregate and stage-specific performance.
