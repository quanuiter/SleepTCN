\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Background.} Sleep staging models are usually developed and evaluated on one public cohort,
and whether the choices that improve them there also hold on other cohorts is rarely tested on data
unused during development.

\noindent
\textbf{Methods.} On the 78-subject Sleep-EDF cassette recordings we reproduced the single-channel
ZleepAnlystNet baseline and compared it, within one protocol, with a residual encoder and temporal
convolutional network (ResNet-1D-TCN) under several input conditions. Transfer without retraining
was tested on the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS), with three hypotheses committed before 500 held-out
participants were processed, and on Sleep-EDF telemetry (22 subjects).

\noindent
\textbf{Results.} On Sleep-EDF the band-pass ResNet-1D-TCN matched the baseline (pooled \mf\ 0.790
versus 0.775) and trained about ten times faster than it. On the 500 held-out SHHS participants it
exceeded the baseline by 0.047 (\ci\ 0.040--0.053), and all three hypotheses were confirmed; the gain
coincided with band-pass filtering, not with the architecture. On telemetry the baseline scored
higher, and the filter edge that helped differed from SHHS. In SHHS, more than 72\% of deep-sleep
(N3) epochs were scored as N2 by every model, underestimating N3 time by about 43 minutes per night,
although the amplitude cue for N3 remained present; model uncertainty did not reveal the affected
recordings.

\noindent
\textbf{Conclusions.} Preprocessing, more than architecture, determined transfer, and its benefit
depended on the target cohort. Pipeline comparisons should be confirmed on held-out data from each
intended target and report training variability and stage-specific errors.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sleep staging; single-channel EEG; temporal convolutional network;
reproducibility; domain shift; pre-specified analysis.

\section{Introduction}

Sleep staging assigns one of five labels, namely W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, to every 30\,s epoch of a
polysomnographic recording. Manual scoring is slow, requires trained staff and is not fully
reproducible: agreement between scorers is lowest for N1~\cite{rosenberg2013aasm}. Deep networks
trained on a single EEG channel now approach expert agreement on public benchmarks, but they are
usually developed and evaluated on one cohort, and benchmark studies report that their errors change
when the recording device, montage or population changes~\cite{deirossi2025sleepyland}. For use
outside the development cohort, what matters is how a model's accuracy, cost and errors carry over.

This study began from ZleepAnlystNet~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}, a single-channel model that
trains fifteen class-specific convolutional encoders, each on the current, previous or next epoch,
and a BiLSTM over their outputs. We reproduced it on the Sleep-EDF cassette recordings, then replaced
its encoder and sequence model by a residual encoder~\cite{he2016resnet} with a temporal
convolutional network (TCN)~\cite{bai2018tcn}, and examined the effect of band-pass filtering and
amplitude normalisation within the new pipeline. We then asked whether the resulting differences
hold on another cohort, using the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS) as the main target and confirming
its result on held-out participants under a protocol committed before their data were processed.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \textbf{Reproduction and a controlled comparison.} The reimplemented baseline came within 0.0072
of the published Sleep-EDF \mf. Within the same folds and protocol, the ResNet-1D-TCN with band-pass
filtering matched or exceeded it while training about ten times faster than this baseline; the
architecture itself, without filtering, gave no detectable gain. The components are established; the
contribution lies in their controlled comparison and in what transfers.
\item \textbf{Confirmed transfer, and its limits.} On 500 held-out SHHS participants the advantage of
the band-pass pipeline over the baseline, over record-wise $z$-scoring and over unfiltered input was
confirmed under a protocol committed beforehand, and the advantage of band-pass input over the
baseline and unfiltered input exceeded the spread of five E4 training seeds. On a second, small target
cohort with the training derivation, however, the baseline scored higher, and the filter edge that
helped differed from SHHS, so the transfer advantage depends on the target.
\item \textbf{A diagnosis of the dominant transfer error.} After transfer to SHHS, most deep-sleep
(N3) epochs were classified as N2 by every pipeline. The amplitude contrast between N3 and N2 was
still present in the target signal, model uncertainty did not flag the affected recordings, and a
label-free amplitude statistic did. Interventions that adapt to the target either failed or traded
this error against other stages.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

DeepSleepNet combined a dual-branch CNN with a BiLSTM~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet}; attention
models~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep,phan2022sleeptransformer}, sequence-to-sequence, compact and
multi-view models~\cite{phan2019seqsleepnet,supratak2020tinysleepnet,phan2022xsleepnet} and models
with longer context or contrastive pre-training~\cite{phan2023lseqsleepnet,lee2024sleepyco} followed.
Differences in dataset version, subject split and preprocessing between these works are large enough
that cross-table comparison cannot replace an ablation inside one protocol~\cite{phan2022review}.
Single-channel systems also differ in input representation~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet},
and a carefully designed conventional pipeline can match complex deep models~\cite{vdd2023traditional}.

Robustness to a new cohort has been sought through diverse training data, as in
U-Sleep~\cite{perslev2021usleep,fiorillo2023usleep}, mixed-cohort networks~\cite{olesen2021mixed} and
montage-agnostic models~\cite{guillot2021robustsleepnet}, and through adaptation to the target, by
fine-tuning on a few labelled recordings~\cite{phan2021transfer}, adversarial
alignment~\cite{heremans2022adversarial,eldele2023adast}, domain generalisation across several
sources~\cite{wang2024sleepdg} or normalisation of spectral statistics at test
time~\cite{gnassounou2025psdnorm}. A recent benchmark found that N3 remained underestimated out of
domain even after multi-cohort training~\cite{deirossi2025sleepyland}. Our study addresses a narrower
single-source question: which pipeline choices determine transfer, what they cost, and whether their
effects hold on data that did not inform them.

Sleep-EDF Expanded is the standard public benchmark for single-channel
EEG~\cite{kemp2000sleepEDF,physionetSleepEDF,pollard2026physionet}. SHHS is a large multi-centre
cohort assembled to study sleep-disordered breathing~\cite{quan1997shhs} and distributed through the
National Sleep Research Resource (NSRR)~\cite{zhang2018nsrr}; it differs from Sleep-EDF in age,
comorbidity, hardware, derivation and label distribution. Deep-sleep scoring uses slow-wave amplitude
criteria~\cite{rechtschaffen1968,berry2017aasm}; version~3 of the AASM manual~\cite{troester2023aasm}
revised respiratory and cardiac rules and kept the N3 slow-wave criterion, so merging stages 3 and 4 into N3
remains the standard mapping. Slow-wave amplitude varies across the
scalp~\cite{massimini2004travelling}, and its relation to N3 scoring in SHHS has been
examined~\cite{davidson2025n3}.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Cohorts and labels}

\textbf{Sleep-EDF (development).} The Sleep Cassette subset of Sleep-EDF Expanded comprises 153
recordings of 78 subjects. We use the Fpz-Cz derivation at 100\,Hz with 30\,s epochs. Stages 3 and 4
are merged into N3; Movement and Unknown epochs keep their position but are excluded from the loss
and all metrics. An evaluation window, applied identically to every cohort, extends from 30\,min
before the first to 30\,min after the last epoch scored N1, N2, N3 or REM; because its anchors use
reference labels, it is a benchmark convention rather than a deployable rule.

\textbf{SHHS Visit 1 (main target).} Participant identifiers were ranked by SHA-256 with seed 42.
Among the first 220, five unlabelled recordings served for adaptation experiments, 15 as a technical
gate and 180 as the \emph{first SHHS1 sample} (age $63.3\pm11.5$ years; 93 men and 87 women); all
analyses of errors and interventions were developed on it. The participants ranked 221--720 formed
the \emph{holdout SHHS1 sample} (500 participants; age $62.8\pm11.5$ years; 249 men and 251 women),
used for no earlier step. The EEG channel is C4-A1 at 125\,Hz, resampled to 100\,Hz with a polyphase
anti-aliasing filter before epoching.

\textbf{Sleep-EDF telemetry (second target).} The telemetry subset of Sleep-EDF Expanded contains 44
recordings of 22 adults with mild difficulty falling asleep, recorded on two nights in a study of
temazepam~\cite{kemp2000sleepEDF}. It shares the Fpz-Cz derivation and scoring group with the
training data but differs in subjects, recorder and population. It was downloaded and examined only
after the analysis plan for it was committed (Section~\ref{sec:protocols}). Hypnograms that started
after or ended before the signal were completed with Unknown epochs, as in the cassette recordings
(Supplementary Section S10).

Table~\ref{tab:class-distribution} reports the label distributions. Both SHHS1 samples contain about
twice the N3 share of Sleep-EDF and a third of its N1 share; telemetry is scored only between lights
off and on and therefore contains less wake.

\subsection{Preprocessing and model configurations}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} and Table~\ref{tab:configs} summarise the configurations. All signal
conditions operate on the continuous calibrated signal in microvolts before epoching:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unfiltered} (E0--E2): no filtering, clipping or rescaling.
\item \textbf{Band-pass only} (E4): zero-phase Butterworth band-pass, order 4, 0.5--30\,Hz.
\item \textbf{Band-pass, clipping and division by 100} (E3): the same band-pass, a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V and division by 100. The clip never activated, so E3 differs from E4 only by a
constant factor, which batch normalisation after the first convolution largely absorbs.
\item \textbf{Record-wise $z$-score} (E6): band-pass and clip, then subtraction of the mean and
division by the standard deviation of the whole recording.
\item \textbf{Windowed $z$-score} (E6R): as E6, but with the mean and standard deviation of a centred
90-minute window. The statistics of E6 span a median of 23\,h of mostly daytime recording in
Sleep-EDF but only the night in bed in SHHS1, so E6 presents SHHS1 inputs at about 1.3 times the
relative scale seen in training (median within-window standard deviation 0.99 against 0.77);
E6R removes this mismatch.
\end{itemize}
E0 is the reimplemented baseline: fifteen CNN branches, one per stage and per current, previous or
next epoch, produce 75 softmax features per epoch for a BiLSTM with 128 hidden units per direction.
E1 replaces the BiLSTM by a non-causal six-block TCN with a 253-epoch receptive field. E2--E6 use a
ResNet-1D encoder that produces 128 features from the current epoch only, followed by the same TCN.
All models are offline: they use future context, and zero-phase filtering uses future samples.
Layer dimensions and hyperparameters are given in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Training}

The 78 subjects are partitioned into ten subject-disjoint folds. In each round one fold is the test
fold, the next is the validation fold and the remainder are training folds; the same partition is
used for every configuration. The epoch encoder is trained with class weights and selected on
validation data, its features are cached, and the sequence model is fitted on the cache and selected
by validation \mf. Every configuration was trained with seeds 42 and 123, and E4 additionally with
seeds 2025, 2027 and 2029. E0 was selected on held-out validation subjects, whereas the original
study selected on a random epoch-wise split of the training data
(Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_e0_original}).

\subsection{Evaluation, statistics and pre-specified protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

On Sleep-EDF, out-of-fold predictions are pooled; the unit of inference is the subject. Target
cohorts are scored with the ten-model ensemble. Pooled \mf\ is computed from summed confusion
matrices and subject-mean \mf\ averages the participants' scores; metrics use five fixed classes,
and a class absent from both the reference and the prediction of a participant counts with F1 of 0
(Supplementary Section S3).
Differences are given with paired subject-cluster bootstrap percentile intervals (10{,}000
resamples)~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}. These intervals treat the trained models as fixed and exclude
variation between training runs, which is reported separately through the training seeds.

The confirmatory protocol for the holdout sample was published in the public repository on 10
October 2026, before any holdout recording was processed. It fixed three seed-42 contrasts of
subject-mean \mf: H1, E3$-$E0; H2, E3$-$E6; H3, E3$-$E2. A hypothesis was confirmed if its
Holm-adjusted~\cite{holm1979} two-sided Wilcoxon~\cite{wilcoxon1945} $p$-value was below 0.05 and its
bootstrap interval lay above zero. Later analyses were each specified in an amendment committed
before they were run and are descriptive; Table~\ref{tab:roles} lists the role of every analysis.
Commit identifiers and GitHub server timestamps are given in Supplementary Sections S3, S5, S6, S10,
S11 and S12.

\subsection{Computational cost}

Inference was timed on one Tesla V100 for one synthetic 100-epoch sequence (median of 300 passes),
and whole-night inference on a laptop processor (Intel Core i5-13500H) for one fold model and the
holdout recording of median length (932 epochs). Training cost is the wall-clock time of training and
validation over ten folds in the seed-123 run, with every configuration trained sequentially on the
same GPU, decomposed into optimiser steps and time per step (Supplementary Section S6).

\subsection{Analyses of the N3 error}

Four descriptive analyses examine the N3 error after transfer. (i) The input of E4 was multiplied by
0.5--2 before the encoder, and batch-normalisation statistics were re-estimated on unlabelled SHHS1
epochs (AdaBN~\cite{li2018adabn}) (A1). (ii) Power spectra of the unfiltered and band-pass inputs were
compared between cohorts (Welch estimate, 20\,s Hann segments). (iii) For every reference N2 and N3
epoch, the standard deviation of the band-pass input was computed, and its ability to separate N3
from N2 was summarised by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and by
applying to SHHS1 the threshold that maximised balanced accuracy on Sleep-EDF. (iv) Three
interventions were evaluated on the first SHHS1 sample against matched controls: EM prior
correction~\cite{saerens2002adjusting} after temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration},
class-weighted training of the sequence model, and the adversarial adaptation method
ADAST~\cite{eldele2023adast}.

Two further analyses ask whether the error can be recognised and reduced without target labels. The
amplitude-guided rule of A3 relabels as N3 every epoch predicted N2 whose band-pass standard deviation
reaches the Sleep-EDF threshold (16.5\,$\mu$V); nothing is fitted on target data. In a gated variant,
developed after the A3 results on the same cohorts, the rule is applied only to recordings whose
\emph{amplitude disagreement}, the share of high-amplitude epochs predicted N2 or N3 that the model
calls N2, exceeds the 95th percentile of the Sleep-EDF recordings; an analogous gate based on the mean
prediction entropy serves as comparator. Because this gate was developed on the cohorts on which it is
reported, those results are exploratory. An independent evaluation on ISRUC-Sleep subgroup I was
specified (A5) but not performed, because the raw ISRUC recordings have an inconsistent amplitude
scale and subject-specific hardware filters (Supplementary Section S11).

\subsection{Separation of the filter edges}

To locate the effect of band-pass filtering, amendment A4 trains two further ResNet-1D-TCN
configurations with one edge of the same filter: high-pass at 0.5\,Hz only (E4HP) and low-pass at
30\,Hz only (E4LP), each order 4, at seeds 42 and 123, with the same folds and recipe as E4, and
scores them on both target cohorts. An edge is said to carry the transfer gain on a cohort if, at both
seeds, its configuration exceeds E2 with an interval above zero and E4 does not exceed it with an
interval above zero. The Sleep-EDF test folds of the 40 runs were evaluated once, after every run had
passed validation (Supplementary Section~S12).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Reproduction and performance on Sleep-EDF}

The reimplemented baseline E0 reached a pooled \mf\ of 0.7754 and an accuracy of 0.8262, against
0.7826 and 0.8409 published for ZleepAnlystNet (Table~\ref{tab:sleepedf-performance}). The band-pass
ResNet-1D-TCN pipelines E3 and E4 scored highest on every metric, with \mf\ of 0.7904 and 0.7891,
which lies within the range of published single-channel results; because evaluation protocols differ
between studies, these values position the pipelines and do not rank them. E3 exceeded E0 by 0.0150
(\ci\ 0.0057--0.0259) in pooled \mf, improving in 49 of 78 subjects. Of the pre-specified stepwise
contrasts, replacing the BiLSTM by the TCN (E1$-$E0, 0.0048) and replacing the encoder (E2$-$E1,
0.0033) had intervals including zero, and band-pass filtering added 0.0070 (E3$-$E2) with an
interval just above zero, improvement in only 37 of 78 subjects and Holm $p=0.90$, so it would not
have met the decision rule used for the holdout sample. E3 exceeded record-wise $z$-scoring by 0.0213
(\ci\ 0.0122--0.0307; Holm $p=0.0012$). All four contrasts kept their sign at seed 123, but E3$-$E6
was then no longer significant (0.0102, Holm $p=0.13$), so on Sleep-EDF only E3$-$E6 at seed 42 met
both criteria (Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_edf_perclass}--\ref{SM-tab:sup_edf_seed_contrasts}).

\subsection{Which filter edge carries the gain}
\label{sec:edges}

The two filter edges acted differently in the two target cohorts (Table~\ref{tab:edges}). On the
holdout sample, low-pass filtering alone reproduced most of the band-pass gain: E4LP exceeded E2 by
0.057 (\ci\ 0.052--0.063) and 0.029 (0.025--0.034) at seeds 42 and 123, that is 87\% and 81\% of

filtering alone scored below the unfiltered model, by 0.032 and 0.024 with intervals below zero. Under
the pre-specified rule neither edge therefore carried the gain alone: the low-pass edge accounted for
most of it, and the high-pass edge added to it only together with the low-pass edge. Neither edge
changed the N3 error, with 71--74\% of N3 epochs classified as N2 by every configuration. On
telemetry the pattern reversed. E4HP exceeded E2 by 0.034 (0.013--0.058) and 0.039 (0.017--0.064)

interval above zero), so the high-pass effect was not confined to the drug nights. On Sleep-EDF the four configurations differed by less than 0.01. Each edge
alone thus lowered the score in one target cohort, whereas the full band-pass scored at least as high
as the unfiltered model in every cohort (Supplementary Section~S12).

\subsection{N3 under-detection after transfer to SHHS1}
\label{sec:n3}

The largest error after transfer to SHHS1 concerned N3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}). For E3, N3 F1 fell
from 0.809 on Sleep-EDF to 0.406 on the first SHHS1 sample, with precision 0.944 but recall 0.258, and
73.1\% of reference N3 epochs were classified as N2, against 18.2\% on Sleep-EDF. An oracle correction
of this cell alone, using the reference labels, would recover 74.5\% of the cross-cohort drop in
pooled \mf; this ranks the error by its weight and is not an achievable improvement. On the holdout
sample the N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate was 72.3\% for E0, 72.7\% for E3, 71.1\% for E4 and 77.1\% for E6,
so the error was shared by every pipeline, and it occurred in stable N3 as well as near stage changes
(Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_perclass_transfer}--\ref{SM-tab:sup_boundary}).

In terms of sleep parameters (post hoc, within the evaluation window), every ensemble underestimated
N3 time on the holdout sample by 42--45\,min per night against a reference mean of 62\,min, with an
intraclass correlation ICC(A,1) of only 0.24--0.29 with the reference. Total sleep time was estimated
within 3--8\,min on average by E3 and E4 (ICC 0.76--0.77) but overestimated by 35\,min by E0. On
telemetry, N3 time differed from the reference by $+5$ to $+10$\,min. The error was not confined to
older or apnoeic participants: N3 recall of E3 was 0.21--0.29 in every age, sex and AHI subgroup, and
the shortfall in N3 time was largest where reference N3 was longest, 53\,min in women and 48\,min
below an AHI of 15 (Supplementary Section S13).

The amplitude contrast on which N3 scoring relies was still present in SHHS1. Within a recording, N3
epochs had 1.43 times the band-pass standard deviation of N2 epochs, against 1.62 in Sleep-EDF, and
the threshold that best separated N3 from N2 on Sleep-EDF (16.5\,$\mu$V) placed 84.0\% of holdout N3
epochs above it and 82.2\% of N2 epochs below it (Supplementary Section S8). The models, which
recognised about a quarter of these N3 epochs, therefore did not use the amplitude cue as a fixed
threshold would. Their decision nevertheless depended on amplitude: multiplying the input of E4 by
0.5--2 moved the N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate monotonically from 93.3\% to 48.6\%, but N3 recall peaked at
0.356 and larger gains shifted N3 epochs to wake, so no global rescaling removed the error
(Supplementary Section S5).

Interventions that adapt to the target did not resolve it. EM prior correction estimated an N3 prior
of 0.03 even from all 500 holdout recordings, against an observed share of 0.13, and eliminated N3
predictions; class weighting raised N3 recall at the expense of N2; AdaBN lowered subject-mean \mf\
by 0.12; and ADAST improved on its matched control by 0.043 on the holdout sample but remained below
E3 and E4 (Supplementary Sections~S2, S5 and S6).

Finally, we examined whether the error can be recognised without labels. The amplitude-guided rule
raised holdout subject-mean \mf\ of E4 by 0.053 (\ci\ 0.048--0.059) but lowered it by 0.077 on
telemetry and by 0.028 on Sleep-EDF, where N3 was not under-detected. Within the holdout sample, the
amplitude disagreement of a recording was strongly related to its N3 recall (Spearman $\rho=-0.92$
for E4), whereas the mean prediction entropy was not ($\rho=-0.12$): the models were confidently
wrong. Applying the rule only where amplitude disagreement exceeded its Sleep-EDF 95th percentile
changed subject-mean \mf\ by $+0.038$ (\ci\ 0.033--0.043) on the holdout sample, $-0.001$ on Sleep-EDF
and $-0.010$ (\ci\ $-0.023$ to 0.000) on telemetry, against $+0.030$ for the entropy gate on the
holdout sample; the other configurations behaved alike (Supplementary Section S11). These gated
results come from the cohorts on which the gate was developed and are not an independent
validation.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What holds}

The reproduction succeeded within a margin compatible with the stricter model selection used here, and
the band-pass ResNet-1D-TCN matched or exceeded the baseline on Sleep-EDF at about a tenth of the
baseline's training time and a third of its inference time. Because every preprocessing variant, seed
and fold has to be retrained, this reduction in cost relative to the reproduced baseline is a practical
advantage, and it does not depend on the target cohort; it was not compared with other compact models.
On SHHS1, the advantage of the band-pass pipeline was confirmed on 500 participants who played no part
in development, and the contrasts of E4 with the baseline and the unfiltered model exceeded the
variation between its five training seeds. Most of this advantage
coincided with band-pass filtering rather than with the architecture, consistent with the
observation that the filter removes the frequency ranges in which SHHS1 and Sleep-EDF differ most.
Separating the two edges showed that, in SHHS1, the low-pass edge accounted for most of the filtering
gain, matching the large spectral difference above 40\,Hz, but that the full band-pass was still
required for all of it. Because SHHS1 resampling includes anti-alias filtering, that difference cannot
be attributed to muscle artefact alone.

Amplitude normalisation did not help. Matching the span of the $z$-score statistics (E6R) removed the
scale mismatch of E6 but not its deficit, and lowered N3 recall further, to 0.140 and 0.152 with
84\% and 83\% of N3 epochs classified as N2. A plausible mechanism is local amplitude compression: in
$z_t=(x_t-\mu_{W_t})/\sigma_{W_t}$, a 90-minute window $W_t$ rich in high-amplitude slow waves has a
larger denominator, which reduces the normalised amplitude of its N3 epochs and thus the cue that
separates N3 from N2 (amplitude ratio 1.62 in Sleep-EDF and 1.43 in SHHS1). Neither the stage-wise
local denominator nor its effect on predictions was measured, so this explanation remains a
hypothesis; the comparison concerns these two forms of $z$-scoring, not every normalisation.

\subsection{The transfer advantage depends on the target}

The telemetry cohort did not reproduce the SHHS1 result. There, the baseline scored highest and
filtering gave no gain. Telemetry shares the derivation and scoring group of the training data, so the out-of-band differences that filtering removes for SHHS1 are not expected, and
the error that dominated SHHS1, N3 under-detection, was absent. The two target cohorts thus represent
different kinds of shift, and a pipeline choice that helps under one did not help under the other.
The filter edges behaved accordingly: on telemetry the high-pass edge alone improved on the
unfiltered model, whereas the low-pass edge alone did not, the reverse of SHHS1. Because each edge
alone lowered the score in one of the two targets, the full band-pass is the safer default when the
target cohort is not known in advance; which low- or high-frequency components are responsible was not
identified.
Telemetry is small, and half of its nights were recorded under temazepam, so it does not establish
that the baseline transfers better in general; it does show that the SHHS1 advantage is not
universal. For practice, the recommendation is not a single pipeline but confirmation on held-out
data from each intended target, together with the variation between training runs.

\subsection{N3 under-detection}

Every pipeline under-detected N3 in SHHS1, and the original ZleepAnlystNet models showed the same
pattern on SHHS recordings from another central derivation~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}. A
benchmark of models trained on many cohorts also reported N3 underestimation out of
domain~\cite{deirossi2025sleepyland}. Our analyses narrow the explanation. The SHHS1 signal was not
smaller than the Sleep-EDF signal, and the amplitude contrast between N3 and N2 was largely preserved,
so the information that scoring rules use was available; yet the models did not exploit it and were
confident in their errors. Spectral and spatial differences between slow waves recorded at C4-A1 and
Fpz-Cz, and differences between scorers, remain plausible contributors that the present design cannot
separate. Methods that adapt to unlabelled target data failed or traded the error against other
stages, and corrections must be applied selectively: the amplitude rule helped where N3 was
under-detected and harmed where it was not. The gated rule suggests that a label-free statistic
grounded in the scoring criterion can recognise this failure where model uncertainty cannot, but it
was developed on the same cohorts and requires independent validation.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study uses one source cohort and two target cohorts. The SHHS1 holdout sample confirms the
findings within SHHS1, and telemetry is small and closely related to the source. An evaluation on
ISRUC-Sleep was planned but abandoned before any prediction was computed, because its raw recordings
proved unsuitable for amplitude-dependent analyses. The amendments after the confirmatory analysis
are descriptive, and the gated rule is exploratory. Bootstrap intervals describe participant sampling
for fixed models, whereas training variability was characterised with five seeds for E4 but two for
the comparators. The two seeds of E2 already differed by 0.0199, more than the largest difference
among the five E4 seeds (0.0189), so the E4 range is not a common uncertainty bound for the baselines,
and neither the tails nor the relative variances of the configurations are established. Adaptation
was not tested on the ResNet-1D-TCN backbone: ADAST raised N3 recall of its own backbone from 0.24 to
0.62 and improved on its control by 0.043, whereas on E4 only AdaBN was evaluated, so whether
adversarial or self-training adaptation would reduce the N3 error on the stronger backbone remains
open; ADAST's gain cannot be added to the score of E4, because the representations, objectives and
budgets differ. No other published architecture, such as a compact sequence model or a model
pre-trained on many cohorts, was retrained within the same folds, so the cost and transfer comparisons
are relative to the reproduced baseline only, and the speed-up reflects the sixteen networks per fold
of that baseline rather than an absolute efficiency. The SHHS1 recordings were resampled from 125 to
100\,Hz with an anti-aliasing filter whose transition band lies above 40\,Hz, so part of the
high-frequency difference between the cohorts, and hence of the low-pass gain, may arise from this
step rather than from hardware or physiology; a training run through the same resampling chain was not
performed. Sleep-EDF is scored with out-of-fold predictions and the targets with ten-model ensembles,
which inflates the apparent cross-cohort drop of a single model by about 0.02. All models use future
context, the evaluation window depends on reference labels, and the subgroup, drug-condition and
sleep-parameter analyses were added post hoc.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

A reproduced ZleepAnlystNet baseline and a ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline were compared under one protocol.
The band-pass ResNet-1D-TCN matched or exceeded the baseline on Sleep-EDF with about a tenth of the
baseline's training time, and its advantage on SHHS1, which coincided mainly with band-pass filtering
rather than with the architecture, was confirmed on 500 held-out participants. On a second target cohort the advantage did not hold, and the filter edge that
helped differed between the two targets; of the filters tested, only the full band-pass did not score
below unfiltered input in either. Under transfer to SHHS1,
every pipeline classified most N3 epochs as N2 although the amplitude cue remained available, and
model uncertainty did not reveal the affected recordings. Pipeline comparisons for sleep staging should
therefore report computational cost, stage-specific errors and training variability, and be confirmed
on held-out data from each intended target.
