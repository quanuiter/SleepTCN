\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Background.} Model and preprocessing choices alter the accuracy, computational cost and
cross-cohort errors of automatic sleep staging, but their effects
are rarely tested on data unused during development.

\noindent
\textbf{Methods.} Six single-channel EEG configurations were trained on 78 Sleep-EDF subjects with
shared subject-wise ten-fold partitions. Transfer without weight updates was examined on 180 Sleep
Heart Health Study Visit~1 (SHHS1) participants and then tested on 500 further SHHS1 participants
under a protocol committed before these data were processed.

\noindent
\textbf{Results.} A one-dimensional residual network with a temporal convolutional network
(ResNet-1D-TCN) on band-pass-filtered input (E3) reached a pooled Sleep-EDF \mf\ of 0.7904. Its forward pass was $3.76\times$ faster than that of a CNN-BiLSTM baseline (E0), despite
$4.37\times$ more parameters. On the 500-participant sample, E3 improved subject-mean \mf\ over E0 by 0.0467
(\ci\ 0.0403-0.0531), over record-wise $z$-scoring (E6) by 0.0293 (0.0231-0.0354) and over the
same model on unfiltered input (E2) by 0.0515 (0.0460-0.0571); all three pre-specified hypotheses
were confirmed. Band-pass-only input (E4), equivalent to E3 under batch normalisation, scored 0.009-0.014 higher,
comparable to differences between two training seeds (0.005-0.020). E0 and E3 classified more than 72\%
of N3 (deep-sleep) epochs as N2. The N2/N3 decision depended on input amplitude, yet rescaling,
batch-normalisation adaptation and adversarial adaptation all failed to remove this error without
losses elsewhere.

\noindent
\textbf{Conclusions.} Band-pass filtering accounted for most of the cross-cohort gain, and
N3-to-N2 confusion persisted in every zero-shot pipeline. Pipeline comparisons should report
variation between training runs and be confirmed on data held out from development.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sleep staging; single-channel EEG; temporal convolutional network;
preprocessing; domain shift; external validation.

\section{Introduction}

Sleep staging assigns one of five labels, namely W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, to every 30\,s epoch of a
polysomnographic recording. Manual scoring is slow, requires trained staff and is not fully reproducible: in the inter-scorer
reliability programme of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, agreement is lowest for
N1~\cite{rosenberg2013aasm}. An
automated system therefore has to be judged not by overall accuracy alone, which is dominated by the
most frequent stages, but by class-balanced metrics, by stability across individuals, and by its
behaviour when the recording device, montage or population changes.

Published results in this field are hard to compare. Studies differ simultaneously in
dataset version, subject partitioning, wake-trimming policy, filtering, normalisation, sequence length
and model-selection rule, which makes architectural effects difficult to separate from other pipeline
differences. For practical development, what matters is which pipeline choices improve
prediction, what they cost computationally, and whether their benefits persist on another cohort.
Answering these questions requires shared evaluation partitions, an explicit account of what changes
between configurations, and evaluation data that did not influence the choices being compared.

We take ZleepAnlystNet~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}, a two-stage single-channel model that couples a
bank of class-specific convolutional encoders to a BiLSTM, as an internal baseline, E0, reimplemented under the present protocol. E1 replaces the
BiLSTM with a temporal convolutional network (TCN)~\cite{bai2018tcn}, reusing the encoder and cached
features but changing the sequence-model training recipe. E2 replaces E1's 75 features, which describe the current, previous and next epochs (C/P/N),
with 128 features from a residual encoder~\cite{he2016resnet} that sees only the current epoch, and
also changes encoder training and selection. Each step therefore compares complete configurations
rather than isolating a single architectural change. Preprocessing contrasts then examine
filtering and amplitude handling within the same ResNet-1D-TCN model and training configuration.

We ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Which of the evaluated changes, to the sequence model, the epoch encoder, signal filtering or
amplitude handling, account for differences in accuracy, computational cost and transfer to another
cohort?
\item Do the resulting differences between pipelines hold on participants who played no part in
development, and are they larger than the variation between training runs?
\item Which sleep-stage errors dominate after transfer, how do they depend on input amplitude, and can
prior correction, class weighting or domain adaptation reduce them without losses in other stages?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions follow these questions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item We show that the implemented ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline requires less computation than the
CNN-BiLSTM baseline despite having more parameters, and that, among the evaluated changes, band-pass
filtering rather than the encoder or sequence model accounts for most of its cross-cohort advantage:
without filtering, the same model performed close to the baseline.
\item We confirm this advantage on 500 held-out SHHS1 participants under a protocol fixed before their
data were processed. We also show that two training seeds of one configuration can differ by as much as
some configuration contrasts, and that the band-pass-only and scaled-input variants are equivalent runs
of one pipeline whose score difference reflects numerical divergence during training; confidence
intervals computed for fixed models therefore understate the uncertainty of pipeline comparisons.
\item We identify N3-to-N2 confusion as a shared cross-cohort error that recurs in every zero-shot
pipeline and in the held-out sample. An input-amplitude sweep shows that this error depends on, but is
not explained by, signal amplitude. In matched experiments, none of prior correction, class weighting,
batch-normalisation adaptation or adversarial domain adaptation reduced this error without losses in
other stages.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

DeepSleepNet combined a dual-branch CNN with a BiLSTM to capture epoch morphology and temporal
dependence~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet}. AttnSleep introduced attention over multi-resolution
features~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep}, and SleepTransformer applied self-attention with explicit
uncertainty quantification~\cite{phan2022sleeptransformer}. Together these establish that sequential
context matters. They also illustrate the comparability problem: differences in dataset version,
subject split and preprocessing between these works are large enough that cross-table comparison
cannot substitute for an ablation carried out inside one protocol, a point emphasised in a recent
review~\cite{phan2022review}.

Single-channel systems also differ in how they represent the signal. SleepInceptionNet
systematically compared raw, spectral and time-frequency inputs and selected a continuous-wavelet,
central-channel Inception model that scores one epoch at a time~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet}.
That study supports two points relevant here: a single EEG channel can support five-stage scoring, and
the choice of input representation is part of the model definition rather than a neutral
implementation detail. Van Der Donckt et al.~\cite{vdd2023traditional} further showed that
increasingly complex deep architectures need not outperform a carefully designed conventional pipeline
under a matched protocol.

Cross-dataset sleep staging is an active methodological problem. Models trained on many cohorts and
channel configurations, such as U-Sleep~\cite{perslev2021usleep} and the mixed-cohort residual networks
of Olesen et al.~\cite{olesen2021mixed}, seek robustness through the diversity of their training data.
Domain-adaptation methods instead adapt to the target domain: He et al.~\cite{he2023crossscenario}
trained a transfer-learning network across Sleep-EDF, SHHS1 and PhysioNet Challenge data, and ADAST uses
domain-specific attention, adversarial alignment and iterative pseudo-label self-training on unlabelled
target data~\cite{eldele2023adast}. Our study addresses a narrower question that these approaches leave
open: within a single-source pipeline, which model and preprocessing choices determine transfer, what
they cost, and whether their effects hold on data that did not inform them.

Sleep-EDF Expanded is the standard public benchmark for single-channel
EEG~\cite{kemp2000sleepEDF,physionetSleepEDF,pollard2026physionet}. The Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS)
is a large multi-centre cohort assembled to study sleep-disordered breathing and cardiovascular
risk~\cite{quan1997shhs}, distributed through the National Sleep Research Resource
(NSRR)~\cite{zhang2018nsrr}. The two differ in age, comorbidity, recording hardware, montage and label
distribution, so SHHS tests a form of cohort change not represented by a held-out split of Sleep-EDF.
We use it to assess robustness to such a change; a retrospective transfer test is not a clinical
validation.

Two further strands of work inform the interpretation. Slow oscillations have a spatially varying
amplitude and propagate across the cortex~\cite{massimini2004travelling}, while deep-sleep scoring uses
amplitude criteria~\cite{rechtschaffen1968,berry2017aasm}. A recent SHHS analysis examined how those
criteria relate to age- and sex-associated differences in N3 scoring~\cite{davidson2025n3}. These studies give context for transfer between derivations and populations; the present experiment
does not establish such a mechanism. Differences in class prevalence can also affect predictions; prior correction
and probability calibration address related but distinct problems~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,guo2017calibration}.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data and labels}

The Sleep Cassette subset of Sleep-EDF Expanded used here comprises 153 recordings from 78 subjects;
we use the Fpz-Cz derivation at 100\,Hz with 30\,s epochs (3{,}000 samples per epoch). Annotations
are mapped to W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, merging the original stages 3 and 4 into N3; Movement and Unknown
epochs retain their temporal position but are excluded from the loss and from all metrics. After
windowing, 195{,}767 epochs remain, of which 195{,}469 are scored and 298 are Movement/Unknown.

An evaluation window, applied identically to both cohorts, extends from 30\,min before the first to
30\,min after the last epoch scored N1, N2, N3 or REM. Because it strongly affects the wake fraction and
every aggregate metric, and because its anchors use reference labels, it is a benchmark convention
rather than a deployable inference rule.

The SHHS Visit-1 selection admitted values 3-7 of the quality field \texttt{overall\_shhs1}, which all
5{,}804 participants in the dataset release met. Participant identifiers were ranked by SHA-256 with
selection seed 42, and the first 220 were assigned roles: 5 adaptation, 15 validation, 180 test and 20
reserve participants. The validation group served as a technical gate: the complete inference run had
to finish without errors on it before the test participants were processed, and no model or setting
was chosen on it. The adaptation group provided the five unlabelled recordings used for prior
estimation and by ADAST. We call the 180 test participants (age $63.3 \pm 11.5$ years; 93 men and 87
women) the \emph{first SHHS1 sample}. It was selected before any prediction was reviewed, but all
subsequent analyses and interventions were developed on it.

To test the main findings on data that had not informed them, we drew a \emph{holdout SHHS1 sample} of
the participants ranked 221 to 720 in the same ranking; one participant without an available recording
was replaced by the next-ranked participant before any processing. The 500 holdout participants (age
$62.8 \pm 11.5$ years; 249 men and 251 women; recordings of 5.5-9.1\,h, median 8.8\,h) had not been
used for any earlier step of the study, and all passed the technical audit. The sample size was set by
the cost of downloading and processing recordings rather than by a power calculation; the confirmatory
intervals had half-widths of about 0.006.

For both samples the EEG channel is C4-A1 at 125\,Hz, resampled to 100\,Hz with a polyphase
anti-aliasing filter (up 4, down 5, Kaiser window $\beta = 5.0$) applied to the continuous signal
before epoching. SHHS labels are mapped $0\rightarrow$W, $1\rightarrow$N1, $2\rightarrow$N2,
$\{3,4\}\rightarrow$N3, $5\rightarrow$REM, $\{6,9\}\rightarrow$excluded. Table~\ref{tab:class-distribution}
reports the three label distributions.

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the evaluated architecture, context and preprocessing
configurations and their shared evaluation protocol. Calibrated EEG is processed continuously
before epoching; the resulting epochs are encoded by either the 15-CNN or ResNet-1D encoder,
then modelled as a sequence by a BiLSTM or TCN.

All conditions operate on the continuous calibrated signal in microvolts before epoching. Four active
signal conditions enter the performance analysis, and one audit-only condition (E5) records a planned
clipping ablation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Raw} (E0-E2): no filtering, no clipping, no rescaling; native $\mu$V retained.
\item \textbf{Band-pass only} (E4): zero-phase Butterworth band-pass, order 4, 0.5-30\,Hz, implemented
as second-order sections with forward-backward filtering.
\item \textbf{Band-pass plus clipping} (E5; audit only): the E4 signal followed by a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V. E5 and E4 inputs were bitwise identical in all 153 Sleep-EDF recordings, so E5 was
cancelled after fold 0, before any score was inspected.
\item \textbf{Band-pass, clipping and division by 100} (E3): the same band-pass, followed by a hard clip
at $\pm800\,\mu$V and division by a constant factor of 100. No record-level or cohort-level statistic
is estimated.
\item \textbf{Per-record $z$-score} (E6): the same band-pass and clip, followed by subtraction of the
mean and division by the standard deviation computed over the whole filtered, clipped recording. This
uses no target labels but does use all samples of the target recording, removes absolute amplitude
differences between recordings and is transductive at the recording level.
\end{itemize}

No notch filter is applied: 50\,Hz mains interference in Sleep-EDF lies at the Nyquist frequency of the
100\,Hz signal, and 60\,Hz interference in the 125\,Hz SHHS recordings is attenuated by the
anti-aliasing filter of the resampling step; both lie outside the 0.5-30\,Hz pass-band.

The $\pm800\,\mu$V clip never activated: band-pass-filtered Sleep-EDF values did not exceed
$246\,\mu$V, every holdout SHHS1 EEG channel has a physical range of $\pm125\,\mu$V, and the
preprocessing audit of the first SHHS1 sample recorded no clipped samples. E3 therefore differs from E4
only by the factor of 100. Because the first ResNet-1D convolution has no bias and is followed by batch
normalisation, this factor is absorbed except through the stabilising constant $\varepsilon$, and the
gradients are unchanged as well, so E3 and E4 differ only through numerical differences during training
(Supplementary Section S4). E3-E2 therefore estimates the effect of band-pass filtering together with
variation between training runs.

The record-wise statistics of E6 are computed over very different spans in the two cohorts. Sleep-EDF
recordings last 17-24\,h (median 23\,h) and include long daytime segments outside the evaluation
window, whereas SHHS1 recordings cover the night in bed. Within the evaluation window, the median
standard deviation of E6 inputs was 0.77 in Sleep-EDF but 0.99 in the holdout SHHS1 sample
(interquartile range 0.85-1.00). E6 thus presents SHHS1 signals to the model at about 1.3 times the
relative scale seen in training, in addition to removing between-recording amplitude differences.

Six configurations, E0-E4 and E6, enter the performance analysis. The baseline encoder concatenates
five-class softmax outputs from 15 CNN branches spanning the current, previous and next epochs (C/P/N),
giving 75 features per epoch; ResNet-1D produces 128 features from the current epoch only. The
non-causal six-block TCN has a 253-epoch receptive field, and the BiLSTM baseline has 128 hidden units per
direction. All models are offline: the sequence models use future context, and zero-phase filtering and
E6 normalisation use future samples. Layer dimensions and hyperparameters are given in Supplementary
Methods S1.

\subsection{Two-stage training and subject-wise cross-validation}

The 78 subjects are partitioned into ten subject-disjoint folds. In each round one fold is the test
fold, the next is the validation fold and the remainder are training folds, so both nights of a subject
share a role and the test fold never enters model selection; the same partition is used for every
configuration. Training has two stages. The epoch encoder uses class weights from the training subjects
and is selected on validation data, by minimum weighted loss for each baseline CNN and maximum \mf\ for
ResNet-1D; its checkpoint then caches features without gradients. The sequence model is fitted on the
frozen cache with masked cross-entropy and selected by validation \mf. E1 reuses the E0 encoder but
changes the sequence-optimiser recipe, and E2 also changes encoder optimisation and selection
(Supplementary Methods S1), so the comparisons fix partitions and evaluation, not every training choice.
Every configuration was trained with seeds 42 and 123. On the same Sleep-EDF Fpz-Cz recordings, E0 at seed
42 reached a pooled \mf\ of 0.7754 and an accuracy of 0.8262, against 0.7826 and 0.8409 reported for
ZleepAnlystNet under ten-fold subject-wise cross-validation~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}, although
E0 was selected on held-out validation subjects rather than on a random epoch-wise split of the
training data (Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_e0_original}).

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

All configurations completed validation-based selection before the seed-42 test predictions were
evaluated. In Sleep-EDF, pooled \mf\ is the headline metric, but the unit of inference is the subject.
A paired subject-cluster bootstrap with 10{,}000 resamples gives percentile intervals for
differences~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}, a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945}
assesses per-subject differences, and Holm correction~\cite{holm1979} is applied over the four
pre-specified comparisons E1-E0, E2-E1, E3-E2 and E3-E6; E3-E0 is post-hoc. Pooled \mf\ is computed
from summed confusion matrices, whereas subject-mean \mf\ averages the participants' scores; metrics use
five fixed classes and assign zero to an empty denominator. Win/tie/loss counts, sign dominance
$(W-L)/(W+L)$ and an exact sign test are descriptive. All intervals are marginal 95\% intervals that
treat the trained models as fixed and therefore exclude variation between training runs. The seed-123
repeat, completed after the seed-42 results were known, is analysed separately.

For SHHS1, each configuration is applied without weight updates (zero-shot): the softmax probabilities
of its ten Sleep-EDF fold models are averaged in float64 before the argmax. On the first SHHS1 sample, the pre-specified primary criterion was
subject-mean \mf, with E3-E0 and E3-E6 as primary comparisons under the same procedure; comparisons
involving E1, E2 and E4 are secondary or post-hoc, and boundary analyses are post-hoc diagnostics
(Supplementary Methods S1).

\subsection{Confirmatory holdout protocol}

Before any holdout recording was preprocessed, a protocol fixing the cohort, preprocessing,
checkpoints, ensemble rule, metrics, hypotheses and decision rule was published in the public project
repository on 10 October 2026, and the analysis script was published there before it was run; the
exact versions are identified in the reproducibility statement. The confirmatory family comprised three seed-42 contrasts of subject-mean \mf, each
with an expected positive sign: H1, E3-E0; H2, E3-E6; and H3, E3-E2. A hypothesis was
confirmed if its Holm-adjusted two-sided Wilcoxon $p$-value was below 0.05 and its paired
subject-cluster bootstrap interval (10{,}000 resamples, seed 2040) lay entirely above zero.

The protocol also specified secondary analyses: the seed-123 replication of H1-H3, E4-E3 together
with the between-seed differences of E0, E3, E4 and E6, ADAST against its matched control, N3 recall and
N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate, a present-class variant of subject \mf\ and the E6 input-scale summary. Twelve
arms were evaluated, E0, E2, E3, E4 and E6 at both seeds plus ADAST and its control, with the locked
checkpoints on CPU, and no metric was computed before all predictions existed. Deviations, none of which
changed a prediction, are listed in Supplementary Section S3.

\subsection{Post-confirmatory amplitude and adaptation analyses}
\label{sec:amendment-methods}

After the confirmatory analysis, an amendment published in the repository before its inference
specified two descriptive analyses of E4 at seed 42, the band-pass-only form of the
pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:holdout}), on the same 500 participants. First, the input was multiplied by
0.5, 0.75, 1.5 or 2 while the encoder kept its stored batch-normalisation statistics, which tests how
strongly the predicted stage depends on signal amplitude. The amendment stated that, if N3
under-detection arises mainly because SHHS1 slow waves reach the encoder at a smaller amplitude than the
training data, N3 recall should rise and the N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate fall for factors above 1, and that a
non-monotonic response would argue against an amplitude-only explanation. Second, adaptive batch
normalisation (AdaBN)~\cite{li2018adabn} replaced the running statistics of the ten batch-normalisation
layers of the encoder, layer by layer, with those of 20{,}000 unlabelled SHHS1 epochs; it is a label-free
adaptation baseline that updates no weights. We also report the per-recording standard deviation of the
filtered input in both cohorts. Conditions were compared with unmodified E4 by paired subject-cluster
bootstrap (10{,}000 resamples, seed 2042) without hypothesis tests. They were computed on a GPU rather
than the CPU named in the amendment; GPU and CPU probabilities differed by at most $1.4\times10^{-5}$
with identical predicted stages (Supplementary Section S5).

\subsection{Intervention experiments}

After characterising the errors on the first SHHS1 sample, we evaluated three interventions on that
sample, each against a matched control and with ten-fold probability averaging: temperature scaling
with EM prior correction for the ten seed-123 E3 models, with priors estimated on the five unlabelled
adaptation recordings; class-weighted training of the TCN on the frozen seed-123 E3 encoder; and
ADAST~\cite{eldele2023adast}, which adapts to unlabelled target recordings, against a source-only model
of the same architecture. ADAST was trained at two learning budgets, 1{,}140 updates per model and 30
complete source-training passes (full-source training), and evaluated at epoch 30. Model selection used
source validation data, and target labels were used only for scoring. The full-source pair was also
applied, without further training, to the holdout sample. Supplementary Methods S2 specifies fitting,
selection, budgets and development experiments.

\subsection{Operational evaluation}

Inference timing was measured on one Tesla V100 for all configurations, excluding I/O, preprocessing
and training: a batch of one synthetic 100-epoch sequence, 20 warm-up passes and three rounds of 100
timed passes in shuffled configuration order, summarised by the median of all 300 passes. The benchmark
measures the implemented forward pipeline for one tensor shape, not whole-night inference time or
algorithmic complexity.

therefore a numerically drifted copy of the corresponding E4 model, and E4-E3 measures how far two
nominally equivalent runs diverge. By contrast, H1 and H3 exceeded every between-seed difference at
both seeds, whereas H2 at seed 123 (0.0129) was close to the between-seed differences of E4 and E6.

\subsection{Cross-model errors and their temporal distribution}
\label{sec:gap}

E3's pooled \mf\ fell from 0.7904 on Sleep-EDF to 0.6099 on the first SHHS1 sample, a difference of
0.1805. Because the two cohorts are evaluated differently, by out-of-fold predictions and by ten-model
ensembles, we examine this difference descriptively by class; A$\rightarrow$B denotes a
reference-labelled A epoch predicted as B. N3 accounts for most of the loss: E3 N3 F1 fell from 0.8085 to
0.4055, with precision 0.9440 but recall 0.2582, and E0 showed nearly the same recall (0.2610) and
N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate (72.3\% versus 73.1\%; Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_first_n3}). The holdout
sample reproduced this pattern: N3$\rightarrow$N2 rates were 72.3\% for E0 (\ci\ 70.1-74.4\%), 72.7\%
for E3 (70.7-74.7\%), 71.1\% for E4 and 77.1\% for E6, with N3 recall of 0.2565, 0.2549, 0.2742 and
0.1987 and N3 precision between 0.85 and 0.88.

In an oracle calculation on E3's first-sample confusion matrix, correcting the N3$\rightarrow$N2 cell
would raise pooled \mf\ to 0.7444, 74.5\% of the cross-cohort difference, against 0.6596 (27.5\%) for
N2$\rightarrow$REM, the second-largest channel; these reference-based figures are neither additive nor
achievable. N3 recall was low not only near reference-label changes (0.0633 for E0 and 0.0733 for E3,
against 0.6121 and 0.6326 on Sleep-EDF) but also in stable regions (0.4008 and 0.3900), and record-wise
$z$-scoring did not remove the deficit (E6 N3 recall 0.2005). Region definitions and all class-wise
results are given in Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_perclass_transfer}-\ref{SM-tab:sup_boundary}.

\subsection{Intervention results}

On the first SHHS1 sample, temperature scaling barely changed subject-mean \mf\ (0.5590 to 0.5595),
whereas adding EM prior correction reduced it to 0.4899 (difference $-0.0696$, \ci\
$[-0.0793,-0.0600]$): the fitted priors placed N3 near the floor, and the ensemble predicted no N3
epochs (Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_calibration_intervention}). Class weighting raised N3 recall
from 0.2345 to 0.3225 (difference 0.0880, \ci\ $[0.0805,0.0954]$) and N3 F1 from 0.3759 to 0.4795, but

unchanged (difference $-0.0016$, \ci\ $[-0.0049,0.0017]$); this intervention acts on the sequence

At the limited budget, ADAST raised N3 recall from 0.3195 to 0.4269 but lowered subject-mean \mf\ from

higher REM recall came with lower REM precision, reducing REM F1 from 0.6231 to 0.5744. Source

reported in Supplementary Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_budget_first},
\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_intervention}, \ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_fullsource_best_summary}
and~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_fullsource_best_classes}.

The holdout sample reproduced the full-source result. ADAST exceeded its control by 0.0429 in

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the pipeline comparisons establish}

The confirmatory evaluation supports three conclusions about the evaluated pipelines. First, the
ResNet-1D-TCN configuration on band-pass-filtered input transferred better to SHHS1 than the
CNN-BiLSTM baseline, and this advantage, first observed on 180 participants, was confirmed on 500 participants whose data had not informed any analysis. It also required less computation: its forward pass
was $3.76\times$ faster in the fixed benchmark and its observed training-and-validation time was
shorter, although the recipes and stopping rules differ. Second, band-pass filtering accounted for most
of this advantage. The same model on unfiltered input performed close to the baseline on the holdout
sample, and on the first sample neither the sequence-model replacement (E1-E0) nor the
feature/context replacement (E2-E1) produced a comparable gain. How the signal is filtered is therefore a substantial part of how these models transfer, not an
implementation detail. Third,
preserving calibrated amplitude (E3) transferred better than record-wise $z$-scoring (E6). The E6
comparison is not a clean test of normalisation as such: its statistics span about 23\,h of mostly
daytime recording in Sleep-EDF but only the night in SHHS1, so the two cohorts reach the model at
different relative scales.

These are comparisons of whole configurations, including their training recipes. We did not test which
frequency band, artefact or recording difference the band-pass filter removes, so the confirmed
contrasts do not identify a mechanism.

\subsection{Training variability and the E3-E4 contrast}

On the holdout sample, two training seeds of an identical configuration differed by 0.005 to 0.012 in
subject-mean \mf\ for the four pre-specified configurations and by 0.020 for E2, and all four
pre-specified between-seed intervals excluded zero. Such intervals
describe the uncertainty due to the sampled participants for fixed models, not the uncertainty due to
training, so a contrast between two separately trained models can appear precise and still be of the
size expected from retraining alone. E4-E3 is an example. The two configurations are equivalent by
construction, and their trained weights show that, for each seed, they are two copies of one training
run that drifted apart numerically; runs that would be identical in exact arithmetic nevertheless
differed by 0.009 to 0.014 in subject-mean \mf\ on SHHS1. No physical or recording-specific mechanism is
needed to explain this difference, and none is implied by our data. The same consideration applies to
the seed-123 estimate of H2, whereas H1 and H3 exceeded every observed between-seed difference.
Reporting variation between training runs alongside participant-level intervals would make pipeline
comparisons more reliable in general.

For practical use, the clipping and scaling steps of E3 are redundant: band-pass filtering alone (E4)
defines the same pipeline with fewer operations, and we recommend that form. E3 remained the reference
configuration of the confirmatory analysis only because the protocol fixed it before the holdout data
were processed. The confirmed contrasts concern the band-pass ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline, of which E3 and
E4 are two realisations.

\subsection{N3 under-detection persists alongside aggregate improvement}

Despite E3's overall advantage, the class-wise analysis shows an error shared by all models. E0 and E3 both
classified more than 72\% of reference N3 epochs as N2 on both SHHS1 samples, with N3 recall near 0.26
despite precision above 0.85; band-pass-only E4 and record-wise $z$-scored E6 showed the same pattern. The oracle
calculation shows the numerical importance of N3$\rightarrow$N2 for E3, and the boundary analysis shows
that the deficit extends beyond the neighbourhoods of reference-label changes into stable N3. E3's better aggregate transfer therefore left the largest stage-specific error in place.
The deficit is not specific to the present implementation: the original ZleepAnlystNet models trained
on Sleep-EDF Fpz-Cz reached an N3 F1 of only 0.43 on 200 SHHS recordings from the C3-A2 channel, a
different derivation from the one used here~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}.

The cohorts differ simultaneously in EEG derivation, population, acquisition and scoring conditions.
Slow-wave spatial structure and amplitude-based N3 scoring criteria motivate examining these
differences~\cite{massimini2004travelling,davidson2025n3}. The amplitude sweep examined one of these
factors directly. The filtered SHHS1 signal was not smaller than the Sleep-EDF signal, yet the
model's N2/N3 decision depended strongly on input amplitude: enlarging the input moved reference N3
epochs from N2 towards N3 and, beyond a factor of 1.5, towards wake. This behaviour is consistent with a
decision boundary that, like the scoring rules, relies on slow-wave amplitude. However, no single factor
recovered N3 without degrading other stages, and the most favourable factor still left 60\% of N3
epochs classified as N2. A global amplitude mismatch is therefore not a sufficient explanation; the
spectral and spatial composition of slow waves recorded at C4-A1 rather than Fpz-Cz, and differences
between scorers, remain plausible contributors that the present design cannot separate.

The interventions changed the N3 error in different ways. EM prior correction eliminated N3
predictions, and class weighting raised N3 recall at the expense of N2 recall and accuracy. ADAST
changed sign between learning budgets; with full-source training it more than doubled N3 recall and
improved on its matched control on both SHHS1 samples, but lowered N1, N2 and REM F1 and source-cohort
performance and remained below zero-shot E3 and E4. AdaBN left N3 recall unchanged and lowered aggregate
performance by shifting the wake and REM boundaries. Cohort-level adaptation therefore either left the
N3 error unchanged or traded it against other stages, and neither label-free adaptation baseline
exceeded the zero-shot band-pass pipeline. The sign change between budgets also shows that the learning
budget must be reported when an unsupervised domain-adaptation baseline is compared, and greater REM
recall without greater REM F1 shows that recall gains must be read together with precision.

\subsection{Positioning and limits of generalisation}

This study combines paired pipeline comparisons, computational cost, an analysis of cross-cohort errors
and a confirmatory evaluation on held-out participants. It complements work on input representations,
conventional and deep pipelines, and multi-cohort training~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet,vdd2023traditional,perslev2021usleep,olesen2021mixed}.
E0 is a reimplementation of the ZleepAnlystNet design under the stated protocol and scored 0.0072 below
the published Sleep-EDF \mf\ of that design. The primary comparisons
evaluate transfer without weight updates. ADAST evaluates target-domain weight updates against a matched
source-only control, and AdaBN evaluates target-domain normalisation statistics without weight updates.

Several limits apply. The study evaluates transfer from one source cohort to one target cohort; the
holdout sample is new in its participants but comes from the same SHHS1 study, montage and scoring
procedures, so it confirms the findings within SHHS1 rather than across further cohorts. The
confirmatory protocol was archived in the authors' public repository rather than in an independent
study registry, so only its existence before the analysis, not the absence of earlier data access, is
externally timestamped. Each
configuration was trained with two seeds, which suffices to show that training variability matters but
not to estimate its distribution. The intervention experiments were designed after the errors of the
first SHHS1 sample had been examined, and only the full-source ADAST pair was re-evaluated on the
holdout sample. The amplitude and AdaBN analyses were specified after the confirmatory results and
examine one configuration at one seed. Sleep-EDF uses out-of-fold predictions and SHHS1 uses ten-model ensembles, so score
differences between the cohorts combine cohort and evaluation-procedure differences. The models use
future context and were evaluated offline, so forward-pass timing describes offline processing rather
than real-time operation. Class-wise results are pooled across participants without sex- or
age-stratified analysis. Evaluation on further cohorts, montages and subgroups would test whether the
filtering effect and the N3 pattern generalise.

\section{Conclusion}

Under a shared protocol, the ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline on band-pass-filtered EEG required less computation
than the CNN-BiLSTM baseline and transferred better from Sleep-EDF to SHHS1. Its advantage, first observed
on 180 participants, was confirmed on 500 participants held out from development: subject-mean \mf\
improved by 0.0467 over the baseline, by 0.0293 over record-wise $z$-scoring and by 0.0515 over the same
model on unfiltered input. Band-pass filtering accounted for most of the transfer gain. Because
clipping never activated and batch normalisation absorbs a constant input scale, the band-pass-only and
scaled variants were two numerically drifted runs of the same model, and band-pass filtering alone
suffices to define the pipeline. All zero-shot pipelines classified most N3 epochs as N2. This decision
depended strongly on input amplitude, but none of rescaling, prior correction, class weighting,
batch-normalisation adaptation or adversarial domain adaptation removed the error without losses in
other stages. Pipeline comparisons for sleep staging should therefore report computational cost, stage-specific errors and variation between training runs,
and should be confirmed on data that did not inform the comparisons.
