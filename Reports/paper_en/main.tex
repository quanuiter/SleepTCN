\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Background.} Model and preprocessing choices alter the accuracy, computational cost and
cross-cohort errors of automatic sleep staging, but their effects are rarely confirmed on data not
used during development.

\noindent
\textbf{Methods.} Six single-channel EEG configurations were trained on 78 Sleep-EDF subjects with
shared subject-wise ten-fold partitions. Transfer without weight updates was examined on 180 Sleep
Heart Health Study Visit~1 (SHHS1) participants and then tested on 500 further SHHS1 participants
under a protocol committed before these data were processed.

\noindent
\textbf{Results.} A one-dimensional residual network with a temporal convolutional network
(ResNet-1D-TCN) on band-pass-filtered input (E3) reached a pooled Sleep-EDF \mf\ of 0.7904. Its
forward pass was $3.76\times$ faster than that of a CNN-BiLSTM baseline (E0), with $4.37\times$ as
many parameters. On the 500-participant sample, E3 improved subject-mean \mf\ over E0 by 0.0467
(\ci\ 0.0403-0.0531), over record-wise $z$-scoring (E6) by 0.0293 (0.0231-0.0354) and over the
same model on unfiltered input (E2) by 0.0515 (0.0460-0.0571); all three pre-specified hypotheses
were confirmed. Band-pass-only input (E4), equivalent to E3 under batch normalisation, scored 0.009-0.014 higher,
comparable to differences between two training seeds (0.005-0.020). E0 and E3 classified more than 72\%
of N3 (deep-sleep) epochs as N2. This decision depended on input amplitude, but rescaling,
batch-normalisation adaptation and adversarial domain adaptation each failed to correct it without
losses in other stages.

\noindent
\textbf{Conclusions.} Band-pass filtering accounted for most of the cross-cohort gain, and
N3-to-N2 confusion persisted in every zero-shot pipeline. Pipeline comparisons should report
variation between training runs and be confirmed on data held out from development.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sleep staging; single-channel EEG; temporal convolutional network;
preprocessing; domain shift; external validation.

\section{Introduction}

Sleep staging assigns one of five labels, namely W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, to every 30\,s epoch of a
polysomnographic recording. Manual scoring is slow, requires trained expertise, and retains
disagreement between scorers; in the inter-scorer reliability programme of the American
Academy of Sleep Medicine, N1 shows the lowest agreement of all stages~\cite{rosenberg2013aasm}. An
automated system therefore has to be judged not by overall accuracy alone, which is dominated by the
most frequent stages, but by class-balanced metrics, by stability across individuals, and by its
behaviour when the recording device, montage or population changes.

Progress in this field is difficult to compare across published tables. Studies differ simultaneously in
dataset version, subject partitioning, wake-trimming policy, filtering, normalisation, sequence length
and model-selection rule, which makes architectural effects difficult to separate from other pipeline
differences. For practical development, the relevant questions are which pipeline choices improve
prediction, what they cost computationally, and whether their benefits persist on another cohort.
Answering these questions requires shared evaluation partitions, an explicit account of what changes
between configurations, and evaluation data that did not influence the choices being compared.

We take ZleepAnlystNet~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}, a two-stage single-channel model that couples a
bank of class-specific convolutional encoders to a BiLSTM, as an internal baseline, E0, reimplemented under the present protocol. E1 replaces the
BiLSTM with a temporal convolutional network (TCN)~\cite{bai2018tcn}, reusing the encoder and cached
features but changing the sequence-model training recipe. E2 replaces E1's 75-dimensional
current/previous/next (C/P/N) feature-context package with a 128-dimensional current-epoch residual
encoder~\cite{he2016resnet}, also changing encoder training and selection. These are comparisons of
specified configurations, not isolated architectural effects. Preprocessing contrasts then examine
filtering and amplitude handling within the same ResNet-1D-TCN model and training configuration.

We ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item How do the evaluated configurations differ in classification performance and in the
computational resources they require?
\item With the model architecture and training settings held fixed, how do filtering and amplitude
normalisation affect performance?
\item When applied to another cohort without updating model weights, which configurations perform
better, does the ranking hold on participants who were not used during development, and which sleep
stages remain difficult to recognise?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item We quantify the trade-off between classification performance and computational cost. The
implemented ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline requires less computation time than the CNN-BiLSTM baseline,
despite having more parameters.
\item We show, on 500 SHHS1 participants held out from all development decisions, that the
cross-cohort advantage of this pipeline over the baseline is confirmed and that band-pass filtering
accounts for most of it: without filtering, the same model performed close to the baseline.
\item We identify N3-to-N2 confusion as a shared cross-cohort error that recurs in every zero-shot
pipeline and in the held-out sample. An input-amplitude sweep shows that this error depends on, but is
not explained by, signal amplitude, and matched experiments quantify how class weighting,
batch-normalisation adaptation and adversarial domain adaptation trade N3 recall against the
recognition of other stages.
\item We show that two training seeds of an identical configuration can differ by as much as some of
the configuration contrasts, and that the band-pass-only and scaled-input variants are equivalent runs
of one pipeline whose score difference reflects numerical divergence during training. Confidence
intervals computed for fixed models therefore understate the uncertainty of pipeline comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

DeepSleepNet combined a dual-branch CNN with a BiLSTM to capture epoch morphology and temporal
dependence~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet}. AttnSleep introduced attention over multi-resolution
features~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep}, and SleepTransformer applied self-attention with explicit
uncertainty quantification~\cite{phan2022sleeptransformer}. Together these establish that sequential
context matters. They also illustrate the comparability problem: differences in dataset version,
subject split and preprocessing between these works are large enough that cross-table comparison
cannot substitute for an ablation carried out inside one protocol, a point emphasised in a recent
review~\cite{phan2022review}.

Single-channel systems also span materially different input representations. SleepInceptionNet
systematically compared raw, spectral and time-frequency inputs and selected a continuous-wavelet,
central-channel Inception model that scores one epoch at a time~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet}.
That study supports two points relevant here: a single EEG channel can support five-stage scoring, and
the choice of input representation is part of the model definition rather than a neutral
implementation detail. Van Der Donckt et al.~\cite{vdd2023traditional} further showed that
increasingly complex deep architectures need not outperform a carefully designed conventional pipeline
under a matched protocol.

Cross-dataset sleep staging is an active methodological problem. Models trained on many cohorts and
channel configurations, such as U-Sleep~\cite{perslev2021usleep} and the mixed-cohort residual networks
of Olesen et al.~\cite{olesen2021mixed}, seek robustness through the diversity of their training data.
Domain-adaptation methods instead adapt to the target domain: He et al.~\cite{he2023crossscenario}
trained a transfer-learning network across Sleep-EDF, SHHS1 and PhysioNet Challenge data, and ADAST uses
domain-specific attention, adversarial alignment and iterative pseudo-label self-training on unlabelled
target data~\cite{eldele2023adast}. Our study addresses a narrower question that these approaches leave
open: within a single-source pipeline, which model and preprocessing choices determine transfer, what
they cost, and whether their effects hold on data that did not inform them.

Sleep-EDF Expanded is the standard public benchmark for single-channel
EEG~\cite{kemp2000sleepEDF,physionetSleepEDF,pollard2026physionet}. The Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS)
is a large multi-centre cohort assembled to study sleep-disordered breathing and cardiovascular
risk~\cite{quan1997shhs}, distributed through the National Sleep Research Resource
(NSRR)~\cite{zhang2018nsrr}. The two differ in age, comorbidity, recording hardware, montage and label
distribution, so SHHS tests a form of cohort change not represented by a held-out split of Sleep-EDF.
We use it to assess cross-cohort robustness, without equating retrospective transfer testing with
clinical validation.

Two further strands of work inform the interpretation. Slow oscillations have a spatially varying
amplitude and propagate across the cortex~\cite{massimini2004travelling}, while deep-sleep scoring uses
amplitude criteria~\cite{rechtschaffen1968,berry2017aasm}. A recent SHHS analysis examined how those
criteria relate to age- and sex-associated differences in N3 scoring~\cite{davidson2025n3}. Such work
provides context for transfer between derivations and populations rather than a mechanism established
by the present experiment. Differences in class prevalence can also affect predictions; prior correction
and probability calibration address related but distinct problems~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,guo2017calibration}.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data and labels}

Our Sleep Cassette analysis sample from Sleep-EDF Expanded comprises 153 recordings from 78 subjects;
we use the Fpz-Cz derivation at 100\,Hz with 30\,s epochs (3{,}000 samples per epoch). Annotations
are mapped to W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, merging the original stages 3 and 4 into N3; Movement and Unknown
epochs retain their temporal position but are excluded from the loss and from all metrics. After
windowing, 195{,}767 epochs remain, of which 195{,}469 are scored and 298 are Movement/Unknown.

An evaluation window is applied identically to both cohorts. It is anchored on the first and last true
sleep epoch of type N1, N2, N3 or REM, and extended by 30\,min of wake on each side, i.e.\ 60 epochs per
side. This policy is stated explicitly because it strongly affects the wake fraction and hence every
aggregate metric, and because it is one of the reasons published numbers are not directly
comparable. It is a benchmark window, not a deployable inference rule, since its anchors use
reference labels.

The SHHS Visit-1 selection used the metadata quality field \texttt{overall\_shhs1}, admitting values
3-7, which all 5{,}804 participants in the dataset release met. Participant identifiers were ranked
deterministically by SHA-256 with selection seed 42, and the first 220 were assigned roles: 5
adaptation, 15 validation, 180 test and 20 reserve participants. In the primary experiments the
adaptation group served technical checks only; the ADAST experiment later used its five unlabelled
recordings for weight updates. No reserve replacements were required. We refer to the 180 test
participants as the \emph{first SHHS1 sample}; it was selected before predictions were reviewed, but all
subsequent analyses and interventions in this study were developed on it.

To test the main findings on data that had not informed them, we drew a \emph{holdout SHHS1 sample}
from the same ranking: participants ranked 221 to 720, which excludes every participant assigned a role
above. One ranked participant had no recording available and was replaced, before any processing, by
the next-ranked participant. The 500 holdout participants (age $62.8 \pm 11.5$ years; 249 men and 251
women) had not been used for preprocessing, inference, model selection, intervention design or
analysis, and all passed the technical audit. Holdout recordings lasted 5.5-9.1\,h (median 8.8\,h).

For both samples the EEG channel is C4-A1 at 125\,Hz, resampled to 100\,Hz with a polyphase
anti-aliasing filter (up 4, down 5, Kaiser window $\beta = 5.0$) applied to the continuous signal
before epoching. SHHS labels are mapped $0\rightarrow$W, $1\rightarrow$N1, $2\rightarrow$N2,
$\{3,4\}\rightarrow$N3, $5\rightarrow$REM, $\{6,9\}\rightarrow$excluded. Table~\ref{tab:class-distribution}
reports the three label distributions.

\subsection{Preprocessing conditions}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the evaluated architecture, context and preprocessing
configurations and their shared evaluation protocol. Calibrated EEG is processed continuously
before epoching; the resulting epochs are encoded by either the 15-CNN or ResNet-1D encoder,
then modelled as a sequence by a BiLSTM or TCN.

All conditions operate on the continuous calibrated signal in microvolts before epoching. Four active
signal conditions enter the performance analysis, and one audit-only condition (E5) records a planned
clipping ablation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Raw} (E0-E2): no filtering, no clipping, no rescaling; native $\mu$V retained.
\item \textbf{Band-pass only} (E4): zero-phase Butterworth band-pass, order 4, 0.5-30\,Hz, implemented
as second-order sections with forward-backward filtering.
\item \textbf{Band-pass plus clipping} (E5; audit only): the E4 signal followed by a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V. Across all 153 Sleep-EDF recordings, E5 and E4 were bitwise identical and the measured clipped-sample fraction was zero. Fold 0 was
retained as an audit record; the remaining folds and all performance tests were cancelled before
scores were inspected because E5 did not define an independent input condition.
\item \textbf{Band-pass, clipping and division by 100} (E3): the same band-pass, followed by a hard clip
at $\pm800\,\mu$V and division by a constant factor of 100. No record-level or cohort-level statistic
is estimated.
\item \textbf{Per-record $z$-score} (E6): the same band-pass and clip, followed by subtraction of the
mean and division by the standard deviation computed over the whole filtered, clipped recording. This
uses no target labels but does use all samples of the target recording, removes absolute amplitude
differences between recordings and is transductive at the recording level.
\end{itemize}

No notch filter is applied: 50\,Hz lies outside the 0.5-30\,Hz pass-band and at the Nyquist frequency
of the 100\,Hz signal.

The $\pm800\,\mu$V clip lies outside the range of both signals: band-pass-filtered Sleep-EDF values
did not exceed $246\,\mu$V, every holdout SHHS1 EEG channel has a physical range of $\pm125\,\mu$V,
and the preprocessing audit of the first SHHS1 sample recorded no clipped samples. Clipping
therefore never activated, and E3 differs from E4 only by the factor of 100. This factor has almost no
effect on the function the ResNet-1D encoder can represent: its first convolution has no bias and is
followed by batch normalisation, which removes a positive constant input scale except through its
stabilising constant $\varepsilon$; dividing the input by 100 is equivalent to multiplying $\varepsilon$
of that layer by $10^{4}$. Because the normalised output does not depend on the input scale, the
gradients with respect to all weights are also unchanged apart from this constant (Supplementary
Section S4). E3 and E4 share seed, initialisation, batch order and optimiser settings, so in exact
arithmetic each E3 run would follow the parameter trajectory of the corresponding E4 run; in practice,
rounding differences let the two runs drift apart, and early stopping can select different epochs.
E3 and E4 are therefore two realisations of the same band-pass pipeline, and E3-E2 estimates the
effect of band-pass filtering together with variation between training runs.

The record-wise statistics of E6 are computed over very different spans in the two cohorts. Sleep-EDF
recordings last 17-24\,h (median 23\,h) and include long daytime segments outside the evaluation
window, whereas SHHS1 recordings cover the night in bed. Within the evaluation window, the median
standard deviation of E6 inputs was 0.77 in Sleep-EDF but 0.99 in the holdout SHHS1 sample
(interquartile range 0.85-1.00). E6 thus presents SHHS1 signals to the model at about 1.3 times the
relative scale seen in training, in addition to removing between-recording amplitude differences.

Six active configurations (E0-E4 and E6) enter performance analysis; E5 documents the cancelled
clipping-only contrast. The baseline encoder concatenates five-class softmax outputs from 15 CNN branches
spanning current, previous and next epochs (C/P/N), giving 75 features per central epoch. Context stays
within each recording, with edge-epoch replication. ResNet-1D produces 128 features from the current
epoch only. The non-causal six-block TCN has a 253-epoch receptive field; the BiLSTM baseline has 128
hidden units per direction. Both sequence models process the retained recording with masked padding.
These are offline models: the BiLSTM and TCN use future context, and zero-phase filtering and E6
normalisation use future signal samples. The forward benchmark is therefore not a measure of online
decision latency. Layer dimensions and all hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Two-stage training and subject-wise cross-validation}

The 78 subjects are partitioned into ten subject-disjoint folds. In each evaluation round one fold is
the test fold, the next fold (modulo 10) is the validation fold and the remainder are training folds.
Both nights of a subject always share the same role. The same partition is used for every
configuration, and the test fold never participates in model selection. The training API accepts only
training and validation loaders, and encoder fitting and feature caching follow the same subject-fold
assignments.

Training has two stages. The epoch encoder uses class weights calculated from training subjects and
is selected on validation data: minimum weighted validation loss for each baseline CNN and maximum
validation \mf\ for ResNet-1D. Its selected checkpoint is hash-verified and used to cache features
without gradients. The sequence model is fitted on the frozen cache using masked cross-entropy and
selected by validation \mf. E1 reuses the E0 encoder checkpoints but changes the sequence optimiser
settings: learning rate 0.01 versus 0.0005, batch size 4 versus 8 recordings, maximum 1{,}000 versus
300 epochs and patience 10 versus 30 for E0 and E1, respectively. E2 additionally changes encoder
optimisation and selection alongside the feature/context replacement. Supplementary Methods S1
provides the complete recipes. The comparisons therefore preserve subject partitions and evaluation,
not every training choice. Every configuration was trained with two fixed seeds, 42 and 123.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

All six configurations completed validation-based selection before the seed-42 test predictions were
evaluated. In Sleep-EDF, pooled \mf\ is the descriptive headline metric, but the unit of statistical
inference is the subject. A paired subject-cluster bootstrap with 10{,}000 resamples estimates
percentile confidence intervals for the difference in pooled \mf~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}, while a
two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} assesses the per-subject differences. Holm
correction~\cite{holm1979} is applied over the four pre-specified comparisons E1-E0, E2-E1,
E3-E2 and E3-E6. Pooled \mf\ is computed from the sum of subject confusion matrices;
subject-mean \mf\ is the arithmetic mean of individual subjects' scores. The two are not
interchangeable, and pooled \mf\ is not a weighted average of individual \mf\ values. All bootstrap
intervals are marginal 95\% intervals, not multiplicity-adjusted simultaneous intervals.
Supplementary Methods S1 specifies bootstrap seeds and zero-difference handling. Metrics use five
fixed classes and assign zero when a class-wise denominator is zero. E3-E0 is post-hoc in the
Sleep-EDF analysis. Win/tie/loss counts, sign dominance $(W-L)/(W+L)$ and an unadjusted exact sign
test are reported as descriptive robustness checks.

Bootstrap intervals and Wilcoxon tests treat the trained models as fixed and do not include variation
between training runs. The seed-123 repeat, completed after the seed-42 results were known, provides two
fixed initialisations per configuration; the two seeds are analysed separately and their $p$-values
are never pooled.

For SHHS1, each configuration uses all ten Sleep-EDF outer-fold checkpoints. Softmax probabilities are
averaged in float64 across folds before the final argmax for each epoch. On the first SHHS1 sample, the
pre-specified primary criterion was subject-mean \mf, with E3-E0 and E3-E6 as the two primary
comparisons under the same bootstrap and Wilcoxon-Holm treatment applied to the subject-mean
difference. Comparisons involving E1, E2 and E4 on that sample are secondary or post-hoc, as labelled.
Reference-label boundary and stable-region analyses are post-hoc offline diagnostics; their masks,
exclusions and supports are defined in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Confirmatory holdout protocol}

Before any holdout recording was preprocessed, a protocol fixing the cohort, preprocessing,
checkpoints, ensemble rule, metrics, hypotheses and decision rule was committed to the project
repository (commit \texttt{a275617}). The analysis script was committed before it was run (commit
\texttt{2019b51}). The confirmatory family comprised three seed-42 contrasts of subject-mean \mf, each
with an expected positive sign: H1, E3-E0; H2, E3-E6; and H3, E3-E2. A hypothesis was
confirmed if its Holm-adjusted two-sided Wilcoxon $p$-value was below 0.05 and its paired
subject-cluster bootstrap interval (10{,}000 resamples, seed 2040) lay entirely above zero.

The protocol also specified secondary analyses without confirmatory status: the seed-123 replication of
H1-H3 as a separate Holm family; E4-E3 at both seeds, reported together with the differences
between the two seeds of E0, E3, E4 and E6 as a reference for training variability; ADAST against its
matched control; N3 recall, precision and N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate; a variant of subject \mf\ averaged over
the classes present in each participant's reference labels; and the label-free E6 input-scale summary
reported above. Twelve arms were evaluated: E0, E2, E3, E4 and E6 at both seeds, ADAST and its control.
All arms used the locked checkpoints and ten-fold probability averaging on CPU, and no metric was
computed until predictions for every arm existed. Deviations from the protocol, none of which changed
any prediction, are listed in Supplementary Section S3.

\subsection{Post-confirmatory amplitude and adaptation analyses}
\label{sec:amendment-methods}

After the confirmatory analysis, an amendment to the holdout protocol was committed to the repository
(commit \texttt{ad61aa0}) before any of its inference was run. It specifies two descriptive analyses on
the same 500 participants for E4 at seed 42, the band-pass-only form of the pipeline; E3 and E4 are
equivalent runs of one pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:holdout}). In the first analysis, every input sample
was multiplied by 0.5, 0.75, 1.5 or 2 before the encoder, which remained in evaluation mode with its
stored batch-normalisation statistics. Because these statistics fix the input scale the model expects,
the sweep tests how strongly the predicted stage, and N3 in particular, depends on signal amplitude.
The amendment stated in advance that, if N3 under-detection arises mainly because SHHS1 slow waves reach
the encoder at a smaller amplitude than the training data, N3 recall should rise and the
N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate fall for factors above 1, and that a non-monotonic response would argue against
an amplitude-only explanation. In the second analysis, adaptive batch normalisation
(AdaBN)~\cite{li2018adabn} replaced the running statistics of all ten batch-normalisation layers of the
encoder, layer by layer, with the statistics of 20{,}000 unlabelled SHHS1 epochs, 40 from each
recording; the affine parameters and the TCN, which uses layer normalisation, were unchanged. AdaBN is a
label-free domain-adaptation baseline that, unlike ADAST, updates no weights. For both cohorts we also
report the per-recording standard deviation of the band-pass-filtered input.

Each condition was compared with unmodified E4 using the paired subject-cluster bootstrap with 10{,}000
resamples (seed 2042), without hypothesis tests, and no scale factor was to be selected or recommended.
The conditions were computed on a cloud GPU from a package that contained the signals only, without
labels or participant identifiers. GPU and CPU probabilities differed by at most $1.4\times10^{-5}$ with
identical predicted stages; this change of device and the other deviations are reported in
Supplementary Section S5.

\subsection{Intervention experiments}

After characterising the SHHS1 errors on the first sample, we evaluated temperature scaling with EM
prior correction, class-weighted TCN training, and ADAST~\cite{eldele2023adast}. Calibration used all
ten seed-123 E3 folds: each temperature was fitted on source validation data and each EM prior on the
five unlabelled adaptation participants, who are disjoint from the test participants. Class weighting
used ten matched seed-123 pairs, with the corresponding E3 encoder frozen in each fold and a newly
trained unweighted control; folds 0-5 were trained on CPU and folds 6-9 on CUDA. ADAST used ten
freshly trained matched pairs at seed 123, each with a source-only control of the same dual-head
architecture. All intervention arms average the ten fold
probabilities and infer complete target recordings before the same scoring mask is applied. Model
selection uses source validation data, adaptation uses only unlabelled target recordings, and target
labels are used only for scoring. Supplementary Methods S2 specifies the fitting, selection and
adaptation budgets.

ADAST was evaluated at two learning budgets: 1{,}140 updates per model, and 30 complete source-training
passes (35{,}670-37{,}620 updates, depending on fold), here called full-source training. Full-source
training also increases target exposure and changes scheduler timing; it is a learning-budget comparison
rather than an isolated source-coverage intervention. At this budget, the two arms of each pair share
initialisation and source batch order. The primary budget comparison uses epoch-30
checkpoints in both arms; checkpoints selected by source-validation \mf\ are a secondary analysis. On
SHHS1, source-only models use source attention and ADAST models use target attention; Sleep-EDF
outer-test predictions use source attention in both arms. All four systems share one set of 10{,}000
participant-bootstrap draws, which also yields an interval for the difference between the two budget
effects. These intervals condition on the fitted models. Development comparisons of learning budgets and ADAST loss
components on source folds 0 and 1 are reported in Supplementary Methods S2. On the holdout sample, the
full-source epoch-30 models were applied without further training, and no holdout recording was used
for adaptation.

\subsection{Operational evaluation}

Inference timing was measured on identical Tesla V100 hardware for all configurations and excludes
I/O, preprocessing and training. Each measurement used a batch of one synthetic 100-epoch sequence,
20 warm-up passes and three rounds of 100 timed forward passes. Configuration order was shuffled in
each round; we report the median over all 300 passes. This benchmark measures the implemented full
forward pipeline for one fixed tensor shape, not wall-clock inference on whole-night recordings or algorithmic
complexity.

The reference-label boundary diagnostic adds temporal detail. For E0 and E3, N3 recall near label
changes is 0.6121/0.6326 on Sleep-EDF but only 0.0633/0.0733 on the first SHHS1 sample. Stable-region
SHHS1 recall remains low at 0.4008/0.3900. N3 under-detection therefore extends beyond the boundary
neighbourhoods. The region definitions and supports are provided in the supplement, including the
epochs omitted from the near-versus-stable comparison.

E6 provides a label-free but transductive sensitivity check. The separately trained record-wise
$z$-score configuration has N3 recall 0.2005, compared with 0.2582 for E3; boundary-neighbourhood
recall is 0.0721 versus 0.0733. N3 under-detection therefore persists under the tested normalisation.
Detailed class-wise, confusion, oracle and transition results are in Supplementary
Tables~\ref{SM-tab:sup_perclass_transfer}-\ref{SM-tab:sup_boundary}.

\subsection{Intervention results}

In the ten-fold calibration experiment on the first SHHS1 sample, temperature scaling changed
subject-mean \mf\ from 0.5590 to 0.5595 (difference $0.00049$, \ci\ $[0.00002,0.00097]$). Adding EM
prior correction reduced it to 0.4899 (difference from temperature-only $-0.0696$,
\ci\ $[-0.0793,-0.0600]$). All ten EM fits converged but placed N3 near the prior floor, and the
ensemble predicted no N3 epochs (Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_calibration_intervention}). This
full-record comparison uses the same ten models in all three arms.

In the ten-fold matched class-weighting comparison, N3 recall increased from 0.2345 to 0.3225
(difference $0.0880$, \ci\ $[0.0805,0.0954]$), and N3 F1 increased from 0.3759 to 0.4795. However, N2
recall decreased from 0.7260 to 0.6728 and accuracy from 0.6892 to 0.6663. Subject-mean \mf\ changed
from 0.5530 to 0.5514 (difference $-0.0016$, \ci\ $[-0.0049,0.0017]$); pooled \mf\ was 0.5930 and
0.5928 for unweighted and weighted training. Class weighting therefore increased N3 recall and F1 at the
cost of N2 recall and accuracy (Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_weighted_intervention}). Within each
fold, both arms share the frozen encoder, initialisation and source batch order, so this is an
intervention on the sequence classifier, not end-to-end retraining.

At the limited budget of 1{,}140 updates, ADAST improved N3 recall from 0.3195 to 0.4269
(difference $0.1074$, \ci\ $[0.0714,0.1422]$) but reduced subject-mean \mf\ from 0.4949 to 0.4497
(difference $-0.0452$, \ci\ $[-0.0558,-0.0347]$). It produced no N1 predictions on SHHS1, and source
out-of-fold pooled \mf\ fell from 0.6665 to 0.5276, so the deterioration was not restricted to the
target cohort (Supplementary Table~\ref{SM-tab:sup_adast_intervention}).

At the full-source budget, ADAST increased subject-mean \mf\ on the first SHHS1 sample from 0.5010 to
0.5455 (difference $0.0445$, \ci\ $[0.0352,0.0539]$), reversing the sign of the limited-budget effect;
the difference between the two budget effects was $0.0897$ ($[0.0767,0.1023]$)
(Table~\ref{tab:adast-budget-main}). N3 recall increased from 0.2432 to 0.6111 and N3 F1 from 0.3869
to 0.7221, whereas N1 recall fell from 0.1568 to 0.1057 and N2 recall from 0.7981 to 0.6369. REM recall
increased from 0.6976 to 0.8360, but REM precision decreased from 0.5631 to 0.4375, lowering REM F1 from
0.6231 to 0.5744. On source outer-test subjects, subject-mean \mf\ was 0.6613 for source-only training
and 0.6281 for ADAST (difference $-0.0332$, $[-0.0434,-0.0229]$). Checkpoints selected by source
validation gave the same pattern (Supplementary

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the pipeline comparisons establish}

The confirmatory evaluation supports three conclusions about the evaluated pipelines. First, the
ResNet-1D-TCN configuration on band-pass-filtered input transferred better to SHHS1 than the
CNN-BiLSTM baseline, and this advantage, first observed on 180 participants, was confirmed on 500
participants who had not informed any analysis. It also required less computation: its forward pass
was $3.76\times$ faster in the fixed benchmark and its observed training-and-validation time was
shorter, although the recipes and stopping rules differ. Second, band-pass filtering accounted for most
of this advantage. The same model on unfiltered input performed close to the baseline on the holdout
sample, and on the first sample neither the sequence-model replacement (E1-E0) nor the
feature/context replacement (E2-E1) produced a comparable gain. Signal handling is therefore a material
part of the evaluated models' transfer behaviour, not a negligible implementation detail. Third,
preserving calibrated amplitude (E3) transferred better than record-wise $z$-scoring (E6). The E6
comparison is not a clean test of normalisation as such: its statistics span about 23\,h of mostly
daytime recording in Sleep-EDF but only the night in SHHS1, so the two cohorts reach the model at
different relative scales.

These are comparisons of configurations, including their training recipes. The study did not test
which frequency band, artefact or recording difference the band-pass filter removes to improve
transfer, and the three confirmed contrasts do not isolate a mechanism.

\subsection{Training variability and the E3-E4 contrast}

On the holdout sample, two training seeds of an identical configuration produced subject-mean \mf\
differences of 0.005 to 0.020 in absolute value, and the intervals of all four pre-specified between-seed
differences excluded zero. Such intervals describe the
uncertainty due to the sampled participants for fixed models; they do not describe the uncertainty due
to training. A contrast between two separately trained models can therefore appear precise and still
be of the size expected from retraining alone. In our data this applies to the comparison of E4 with
E3. E4 scored higher at both seeds and on both SHHS1 samples, by 0.009 to 0.014; on the holdout sample
this difference exceeded the pre-specified between-seed differences at seed 42 but not at seed 123.
The two configurations are equivalent by construction, because clipping never activated and batch
normalisation absorbs the constant factor of 100 except through a stabilising constant that was
negligible in practice. Their trained weights confirm that, for each seed, E3 and E4 are two copies of
one training run that drifted apart numerically. E4-E3 is therefore itself a measure of training
variability: runs that would be identical in exact arithmetic, apart from a negligible constant,
differed by 0.009 to 0.014 in subject-mean \mf\ on SHHS1, and early stopping selected different
epochs for them in 8 of 20 seed-fold pairs. No physical or
recording-specific mechanism is needed to explain the difference, and none is implied by our data. The
same consideration applies to the seed-123 estimate of H2 (0.0129), whereas H1 and H3 exceeded every
observed between-seed difference. Reporting variation between training runs alongside
participant-level intervals would make pipeline comparisons more reliable in general.

For practical use, the clipping and scaling steps of E3 are redundant: band-pass filtering alone (E4)
defines the same pipeline with fewer operations, and we recommend that form. E3 remained the reference
configuration of the confirmatory analysis only because the protocol fixed it before the holdout data
were processed. The confirmed contrasts concern the band-pass ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline, of which E3 and
E4 are two realisations.

\subsection{N3 under-detection persists alongside aggregate improvement}

The class-wise analysis reveals a shared failure mode alongside E3's overall advantage. E0 and E3 both
classified more than 72\% of reference N3 epochs as N2 on both SHHS1 samples, with N3 recall near 0.26
despite precision above 0.85; band-pass-only E4 and record-wise $z$-scored E6 showed the same pattern. The oracle
calculation shows the numerical importance of N3$\rightarrow$N2 for E3, and the boundary analysis shows
that the deficit extends beyond the neighbourhoods of reference-label changes into stable N3. The aggregate
transfer gain of E3 therefore did not resolve the dominant stage-specific error.

The cohorts differ simultaneously in EEG derivation, population, acquisition and scoring conditions.
Slow-wave spatial structure and amplitude-based N3 scoring criteria motivate examining these
differences~\cite{massimini2004travelling,davidson2025n3}. The amplitude sweep examined one of these
factors directly. The filtered SHHS1 signal was not smaller than the Sleep-EDF signal, yet the
model's N2/N3 decision depended strongly on input amplitude: enlarging the input moved reference N3
epochs from N2 towards N3 and, beyond a factor of 1.5, towards wake. This behaviour is consistent with a
decision boundary that, like the scoring rules, relies on slow-wave amplitude. However, no single factor
recovered N3 without degrading other stages, and the most favourable factor still left 60\% of N3
epochs classified as N2. A global amplitude mismatch is therefore not a sufficient explanation; the
spectral and spatial composition of slow waves recorded at C4-A1 rather than Fpz-Cz, and differences
between scorers, remain plausible contributors that the present design cannot separate.

The intervention experiments show different responses to attempts to increase N3 detection. EM prior
correction converged to priors near zero for N3 and eliminated N3 predictions. Class weighting increased
N3 recall and F1 but reduced N2 recall and accuracy, and the interval for its subject-mean \mf\
difference included zero.
Limited-budget ADAST increased N3 recall but reduced aggregate \mf\ and eliminated target N1
predictions. With full-source training, ADAST improved subject-mean \mf\ over its matched control on
both SHHS1 samples and more than doubled N3 recall, while lowering N1, N2 and REM F1 and source-cohort
performance. It nevertheless remained below zero-shot E3 and E4 on the holdout sample. Re-estimating
the batch-normalisation statistics on unlabelled SHHS1 data with AdaBN left N3 recall unchanged and
lowered aggregate performance by shifting the wake and REM boundaries, so neither label-free adaptation
baseline exceeded the zero-shot band-pass pipeline. Cohort-level adaptation therefore either left the
N3 error unchanged, as with AdaBN, or traded it against other stages, as with ADAST. The sign change
of the adaptation effect between budgets shows that the learning budget must be reported when an
unsupervised domain-adaptation baseline is compared, and greater REM recall without greater REM F1
shows that recall gains need to be read together with precision and other-stage errors.

\subsection{Positioning and limits of generalisation}

This study connects paired pipeline comparisons with computational trade-offs, cross-cohort error
structure and a confirmatory evaluation on held-out participants, complementing work on input
representations, conventional and deep pipelines, and multi-cohort
training~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet,vdd2023traditional,perslev2021usleep,olesen2021mixed}.
E0 is a reimplementation of the ZleepAnlystNet design under the stated protocol. The primary comparisons
evaluate transfer without weight updates. ADAST evaluates target-domain weight updates against a matched
source-only control, and AdaBN evaluates target-domain normalisation statistics without weight updates.

Several limits apply. The study evaluates transfer from one source cohort to one target cohort; the
holdout sample is new in its participants but comes from the same SHHS1 study, montage and scoring
procedures, so it confirms the findings within SHHS1 rather than across further cohorts. Each
configuration was trained with two seeds, which suffices to show that training variability matters but
not to estimate its distribution. The intervention experiments were designed after the errors of the
first SHHS1 sample had been examined, and only the full-source ADAST pair was re-evaluated on the
holdout sample. The amplitude and AdaBN analyses were specified after the confirmatory results and
examine one configuration at one seed. Sleep-EDF uses out-of-fold predictions and SHHS1 uses ten-model ensembles, so score
differences between the cohorts combine cohort and evaluation-procedure differences. The models use
future context and were evaluated offline, so forward-pass timing describes offline processing rather
than real-time operation. Class-wise results are pooled across participants without sex- or
age-stratified analysis. Evaluation on further cohorts, montages and subgroups would test whether the
filtering effect and the N3 pattern generalise.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Under a shared protocol, the ResNet-1D-TCN pipeline on band-pass-filtered EEG required less computation
than the CNN-BiLSTM baseline and transferred better from Sleep-EDF to SHHS1. Its advantage, first observed
on 180 participants, was confirmed on 500 participants held out from development: subject-mean \mf\
improved by 0.0467 over the baseline, by 0.0293 over record-wise $z$-scoring and by 0.0515 over the same
model on unfiltered input. Band-pass filtering accounted for most of the transfer gain. Because
clipping never activated and batch normalisation absorbs a constant input scale, the band-pass-only and
scaled variants were two numerically drifted runs of the same model, and band-pass filtering alone
suffices to define the pipeline. All zero-shot pipelines classified most N3 epochs as N2. This decision
depended strongly on input amplitude, but none of rescaling, class weighting, batch-normalisation
adaptation or adversarial domain adaptation removed the error without losses in other stages. Pipeline comparisons for sleep staging
should therefore report computational cost, stage-specific errors and variation between training runs,
and should be confirmed on data that did not inform the comparisons.
