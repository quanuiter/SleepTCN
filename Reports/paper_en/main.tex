\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Background.} Cross-cohort losses are difficult to attribute when architecture and preprocessing
change together. We tested which pipeline choices remained useful before cross-cohort adaptation.

\noindent
\textbf{Methods.} Six configurations were compared on Sleep-EDF Expanded (78 subjects) under shared
10-fold partitions and selection rules. E1 changed only the sequence model;
E2 replaced E1's 75-dimensional C/P/N feature-context package with a 128-dimensional current-epoch
ResNet-1D encoder. Paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon--Holm quantified uncertainty. Transfer used
180 locked SHHS1 participants without weight updates. E6 used statistics from each complete unlabelled
target recording and is therefore label-free but transductive.

\noindent
\textbf{Results.} E3 attained the highest Sleep-EDF \mf\ (0.7904) and exceeded E6 by 0.0213
(\ci\ $[0.0122,0.0307]$; Holm $p=0.0012$); a second seed preserved the direction but not Holm
significance. Other contrasts were small and non-significant. E3 provided a $3.76\times$
forward-pass speed-up over the CNN--BiLSTM control, with $4.37\times$ as many
parameters and 28.4\% more peak memory. On SHHS1, E3 exceeded the control by 0.0412 subject-mean \mf\
(\ci\ $[0.0314,0.0512]$) and E6 by 0.0274. E0 and E3 nevertheless misclassified 72.3\% and 73.1\% of
N3 epochs as N2 and had similar N3 recall (0.2610 and 0.2582); E6 reduced recall to 0.2005.

\noindent
\textbf{Conclusions.} The substitutions offered operational but no stable predictive gains. In this
post-hoc comparison, preprocessing was the stronger development axis. N2--N3 and N2--REM should be
prioritised for labelled adaptation; target-record normalisation alone was insufficient.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sleep staging; single-channel EEG; temporal convolutional network;
domain shift; target-domain adaptation; paired inference.

\section{Introduction}

Sleep staging assigns one of five labels---W, N1, N2, N3 and REM---to every 30\,s epoch of a
polysomnographic recording. Manual scoring is slow, requires trained expertise, and retains
irreducible disagreement between scorers; in the inter-scorer reliability programme of the American
Academy of Sleep Medicine, N1 shows the lowest agreement of all stages~\cite{rosenberg2013aasm}. An
automated system therefore has to be judged not by overall accuracy alone, which is dominated by the
most frequent stages, but by class-balanced metrics, by stability across individuals, and by its
behaviour when the recording device, montage or population changes.

Progress in this field is difficult to read from published tables. Studies differ simultaneously in
dataset version, subject partitioning, wake-trimming policy, filtering, normalisation, sequence length
and model-selection rule, so a reported improvement cannot be attributed to the architecture that the
paper nominally proposes. This creates a concrete development problem: before collecting target-domain
labels, a practitioner cannot tell whether a new encoder, a new sequence model or a change in signal
handling is the more defensible use of effort. A comparison in which one component changes at a time is
needed to separate these decisions.

We take ZleepAnlystNet~\cite{jadhav2024zleepanlystnet}, a two-stage single-channel model that couples a
bank of class-specific convolutional encoders to a BiLSTM, as an internal control. E1 isolates the
replacement of the BiLSTM by a temporal convolutional network (TCN)~\cite{bai2018tcn}. E2 is not an
isolated encoder contrast: it replaces E1's 75-dimensional current/previous/next (C/P/N) feature-context
package with a 128-dimensional current-epoch one-dimensional residual encoder~\cite{he2016resnet}.
We additionally treat signal processing as an experimental factor because filtering and record-wise
normalisation can change the information available to a downstream classifier.

The study is organised around three deployment-oriented questions. (i) Under a shared subject-wise
protocol, do the isolated TCN substitution and bundled E2 feature/context replacement provide benefits
large and stable enough to justify changing the pipeline? (ii) Which evaluated pipeline and preprocessing choices retain
their benefit under label-free cross-cohort transfer to an independent cohort with a different EEG derivation and age
distribution? (iii) When transfer performance falls, which class transitions account for the loss and
do they point to a generic preprocessing remedy or to targeted adaptation?

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item A leakage-controlled 10-fold subject-wise protocol with an isolated sequence-model contrast, a
clearly labelled bundled feature/context replacement, and preprocessing contrasts under shared
partitions and model-selection rules.
\item A locked cross-cohort evaluation on 180 SHHS Visit-1 participants, showing that the ranking and
magnitude of design choices can differ between internal validation and cross-cohort use.
\item A class- and confusion-channel analysis showing that under-detection of N3 as N2 recurs in both
the 15-CNN--BiLSTM control and ResNet-1D--TCN, while over-detection of REM from N2 is a second E3
priority. The accompanying E6 sensitivity analysis shows that per-record normalisation alone does not
resolve the cross-model N3 failure.
\item A quantified operational trade-off: the implemented ResNet-1D--TCN pipeline reduces measured
forward latency and training-and-validation wall-clock time relative to the 15-branch CNN--BiLSTM
control, while increasing parameter count and peak memory and without establishing predictive
superiority.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, we distinguish pre-specified comparisons, secondary evidence and post-hoc contrasts, and we
confine claims to the evidence class that supports them.

\section{Related work}

DeepSleepNet combined a dual-branch CNN with a BiLSTM to capture epoch morphology and temporal
dependence~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet}. AttnSleep introduced attention over multi-resolution
features~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep}, and SleepTransformer applied self-attention with explicit
uncertainty quantification~\cite{phan2022sleeptransformer}. Together these establish that sequential
context matters substantially. They also illustrate the comparability problem: differences in dataset
version, subject split and preprocessing between these works are large enough that cross-table
comparison cannot substitute for an ablation carried out inside one protocol, a point emphasised in
recent reviews of the field~\cite{phan2022review}.

Single-channel systems also span materially different input representations. SleepInceptionNet
systematically compared raw, spectral and time--frequency inputs and selected a continuous-wavelet,
central-channel Inception model that scores one epoch at a time~\cite{haghayegh2023sleepinceptionnet}.
That study reinforces two points relevant here: a single EEG channel can support five-stage scoring, and
preprocessing choice is part of the model definition rather than a neutral implementation detail.

Cross-dataset sleep staging is already an active methodological problem. For example, He et
al.~\cite{he2023crossscenario} trained a domain-adaptation network across Sleep-EDF, SHHS1 and the
PhysioNet Challenge data. ADAST instead uses domain-specific attention, adversarial alignment and
iterative pseudo-label self-training on unlabelled target data~\cite{eldele2023adast}. These approaches
adapt model representations or decision functions using the target domain; our label-free E6 condition
only estimates recording-level normalisation statistics and does not implement domain adaptation.
Van Der Donckt et al.~\cite{vdd2023traditional} further showed that increasingly complex deep
architectures need not outperform a carefully designed conventional pipeline under a matched protocol.
Our study addresses the decision immediately before a labelled adaptation study: which existing
pipeline choices remain useful, and which class boundaries should receive limited target-domain labels?

Sleep-EDF Expanded is the standard public benchmark for single-channel
EEG~\cite{kemp2000sleepEDF,physionetSleepEDF}. The Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS) is a large
multi-centre cohort assembled to study sleep-disordered breathing and cardiovascular
risk~\cite{quan1997shhs}, distributed through the National Sleep Research Resource
(NSRR)~\cite{zhang2018nsrr}. The two differ in age, comorbidity, recording hardware, montage and label
distribution. This makes SHHS a considerably more realistic transfer test than a held-out split of the
same corpus, and it is the reason we treat the transfer result---not the in-domain ranking---as the
principal evidence about deployability.

Two threads of the literature bear directly on our findings. First, the sleep slow oscillation has a
pronounced anterior amplitude maximum and propagates as a travelling wave~\cite{massimini2004travelling},
so the microvolt amplitude of slow-wave activity is derivation-dependent; and slow-wave amplitude
declines markedly with age~\cite{ohayon2004metaanalysis}. Both scoring
manuals~\cite{rechtschaffen1968,berry2017aasm} define deep sleep partly through an absolute amplitude
criterion, which means an amplitude criterion learned on one derivation and one age band need not
transfer. Second, classifier outputs trained under one label prior are miscalibrated under another, and
the correction is well characterised~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,guo2017calibration}. These observations
motivate the class-wise transfer analysis in Section~\ref{sec:gap}; because cohort and derivation change
together, they are treated as candidate explanations rather than mechanisms identified by this design.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data and labels}

The Sleep Cassette subset of Sleep-EDF Expanded comprises 153 recordings from 78 subjects; we use the
Fpz--Cz derivation at 100\,Hz with 30\,s epochs (3{,}000 samples per epoch). Annotations are mapped to
W, N1, N2, N3 and REM; Movement and Unknown epochs retain their temporal position but are excluded from
the loss and from all metrics. After windowing, 195{,}767 epochs remain, of which 195{,}469 are scored
and 298 are Movement/Unknown.

An evaluation window is applied identically to both cohorts. It is anchored on the first and last true
sleep epoch (any of N1, N2, N3, REM) and extended by 30\,min of wake on each side, i.e.\ 60 epochs per
side. This policy is stated explicitly because it strongly affects the wake fraction and hence every
aggregate metric, and because it is one of the principal reasons published numbers are not directly
comparable. It is a benchmark window, not a deployable inference rule, since its anchors use ground-truth
labels.

The SHHS Visit-1 sample was locked before any prediction was inspected and comprises 180 test
participants, with a further 5 adaptation and 15 validation participants that were never used to update
weights. The EEG channel is C4--A1 at 125\,Hz, resampled to 100\,Hz with a polyphase anti-aliasing
filter (up 4, down 5, Kaiser window $\beta = 5.0$) applied to the continuous signal before epoching.
SHHS labels are mapped $0\rightarrow$W, $1\rightarrow$N1, $2\rightarrow$N2, $\{3,4\}\rightarrow$N3,
$5\rightarrow$REM, $\{6,9\}\rightarrow$excluded. Table~\ref{tab:class-distribution} reports both label
distributions.

\subsection{Preprocessing conditions}

All conditions operate on the continuous calibrated signal in microvolts before epoching. Four active
signal conditions enter the performance analysis, and one audit-only condition (E5) records a planned
clipping ablation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Raw} (E0--E2): no filtering, no clipping, no rescaling; native $\mu$V retained.
\item \textbf{Band-pass only} (E4): zero-phase Butterworth band-pass, order 4, 0.5--30\,Hz, implemented
as second-order sections with forward--backward filtering.
\item \textbf{Band-pass plus clipping} (E5; audit only): the E4 signal followed by a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V. Across all 153 Sleep-EDF recordings, E5 and E4 were bitwise identical and the measured
clipped-sample fraction was zero. Fold 0 was retained as an audit artifact; the remaining folds and all
performance tests were cancelled before scores were inspected because E5 did not define an independent
input condition.
\item \textbf{Fixed-scale amplitude handling} (E3): the same band-pass, followed by a hard clip at
$\pm800\,\mu$V and division by a constant factor of 100. The clip threshold is an absolute constant
identical for every recording, so no record-level or cohort-level statistic is estimated and
cross-subject leakage is not introduced; relative amplitude between recordings is preserved.
\item \textbf{Per-record $z$-score} (E6): the same band-pass and clip, followed by subtraction of the
mean and division by the standard deviation computed over the whole filtered, clipped recording. This
uses no target labels but does use all samples of the target recording, removes absolute amplitude
differences between recordings and is transductive at the recording level rather than purely inductive.
\end{itemize}

No notch filter is applied: 50\,Hz lies outside the 0.5--30\,Hz pass-band and at the Nyquist frequency
of the 100\,Hz signal. The contrast E3\,--\,E6 therefore evaluates a practical preprocessing choice:
preserving calibrated cross-recording amplitude relationships or applying record-wise normalisation.
Because clipping never activated in E5, E3 and E4 differ in these data by the fixed division by 100;
this identity is used only to interpret the observed contrast and does not establish equivalence or a
hardware-independent scaling rule.

Six active configurations (E0--E4 and E6) enter performance analysis; E5 documents the cancelled
clipping-only contrast. The control encoder produces 75 features from 15 CNN branches spanning current,
previous and next epochs (C/P/N). ResNet-1D produces 128 features from the current epoch only. The
non-causal six-block TCN has a 253-epoch receptive field; the BiLSTM control has 128 hidden units per
direction. Both sequence models process the full recording with masked padding. Layer dimensions and
all hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Two-stage training and subject-wise cross-validation}

The 78 subjects are partitioned into 10 subject-disjoint folds. In each evaluation round one fold is the
test fold, the next fold (modulo 10) is the validation fold and the remainder are training folds. Both
nights of a subject always share the same role. The same partition is used for every configuration, and
the test fold never participates in model selection; the training API accepts only training and
validation loaders, so test data cannot enter fitting.

Training has two stages. The epoch encoder is fitted with class weights calculated from training
subjects, selected on validation data, hash-verified and used to cache features without gradients. The
sequence model is then fitted on the frozen cache using masked cross-entropy and selected by validation
\mf. E1 reuses E0 encoder checkpoints, so E1\,--\,E0 isolates the sequence-model substitution. In
contrast, E2\,--\,E1 changes the encoder, removes explicit P/N inputs and changes the feature dimension
from 75 to 128; it is a bundled feature/context replacement. Optimisers, learning rates, batch sizes and
early-stopping settings are in Supplementary Methods S1.

\subsection{Test locking, statistical analysis and effect sizes}

All six configurations completed validation-based selection before the test set was opened, once. In
Sleep-EDF, pooled \mf\ is the descriptive headline metric, but the unit of statistical inference is the
subject. Paired subject-cluster bootstrap with 10{,}000 resamples estimates confidence intervals for the
difference in pooled \mf, while a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} assesses the
subject-level distribution of per-subject differences~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}. Holm
correction~\cite{holm1979} is applied over the four pre-specified comparisons E1\,--\,E0, E2\,--\,E1,
E3\,--\,E2 and E3\,--\,E6. These two procedures target different estimands---a pooled, epoch-weighted
quantity and a subject-averaged rank quantity---and may therefore disagree; where they do, we say so
explicitly rather than reporting only the favourable one. E3\,--\,E0 is labelled post-hoc throughout.

We also report win/tie/loss counts, sign dominance $(W-L)/(W+L)$ and an unadjusted exact sign test as
descriptive robustness checks; they are not part of the pre-specified inferential family.

After the primary results had been observed, the whole campaign was repeated with a second fixed
training seed on the same partition. The two seeds are analysed separately; $p$-values are never pooled,
and two fixed seeds are not treated as a sample from the distribution over initialisations. This repeat
is reported as a post-protocol sensitivity analysis.

For SHHS, each configuration uses all ten Sleep-EDF outer-fold checkpoints. Softmax probabilities are
averaged in float64 across folds before the final argmax for each epoch. The pre-locked primary criterion
is subject-mean \mf, with E3\,--\,E0 and E3\,--\,E6 as the two locked comparisons under the same
bootstrap and Wilcoxon--Holm treatment. The E1/E2 component analysis is secondary evidence on an
already-opened sample, and E3\,--\,E2 is explicitly post-hoc.

\subsection{Operational evaluation}

Inference timing was measured on identical hardware (Tesla V100) for all configurations and excludes
I/O, preprocessing and training. Each measurement used a batch of one synthetic 100-epoch sequence,
20 warm-up passes and three rounds of 100 timed forward passes. Configuration order was shuffled in
each round; we report the median over all 300 passes. This benchmark measures the implemented full
forward pipeline for one fixed tensor shape, not overnight wall-clock inference or algorithmic
complexity.

Only E3\,--\,E6 is consistent across all three lenses: a fully positive bootstrap interval, Holm
significance, and a sign test that agrees. The remaining pre-specified contrasts have intervals
containing zero and do not reach significance.

E3\,--\,E2 deserves particular attention because it is the clearest case of estimand disagreement in the
study. Its pooled bootstrap interval marginally excludes zero ($[0.000305, 0.014520]$), yet only 37 of
78 subjects improve, $\dsign = -0.051$, and both the Wilcoxon and sign tests are far from significance
($p_{\mathrm{sign}} = 0.734$). Pooled \mf\ is epoch-weighted and is therefore dominated by subjects
contributing more scored epochs; the subject-level statistics weight every subject equally. When these
disagree, the honest reading is that the effect is carried by a subset of high-epoch-count subjects and
is not a general property of the population. We do not claim E3 improves on E2 in-domain.

positive interval; because it changes architecture and preprocessing simultaneously, it supports the
pipeline as a whole and cannot attribute credit to any component.

\subsection{Per-class and seed-sensitivity checks}

Detailed per-class metrics, the Sleep-EDF confusion matrix and both seed-sensitivity tables are provided
in Supplementary Tables S1--S4. In domain, N1 is E3's hardest class and the largest E3--E6 F1
difference is on N3 ($+0.0628$). All four pre-specified differences remain positive under seed 123, but
only E3\,--\,E6 has a fully positive bootstrap interval in both runs; its Holm-adjusted result is
significant for seed 42 only and its magnitude falls from 0.0213 to 0.0102. Thus, the direction is stable
across these two fixed seeds, but the inferential strength is not; two seeds are not an independent
replication or an estimate of initialisation variance.

\subsection{Feature geometry}

Across all ten folds without exception, the ResNet-1D embedding has a lower silhouette coefficient than
the 15-branch CNN feature vector: mean $0.1048$ (SD $0.0229$) versus $0.2126$ (SD $0.0356$), mean
difference $-0.1079$ (SD $0.0228$; range $-0.1526$ to $-0.0608$). The consistency is total (10/10 folds; Figure~\ref{fig:supporting}a),
so the effect is not noise.

The correct reading is not that the ResNet embedding is worse. Silhouette rewards compact, Euclidean,
per-epoch clusters, whereas the downstream TCN integrates a $\pm126$-epoch neighbourhood and can exploit
distributional or temporal structure that no per-epoch clustering index measures. The 15-branch CNN's
features are, by construction, 75 calibrated class posteriors---a representation almost engineered to
score well on silhouette---while the ResNet's 128 dimensions are unconstrained. That E2 nonetheless
matches or exceeds E1 downstream (Table~\ref{tab:sleepedf-performance}) is direct evidence that
per-epoch cluster separability is a poor proxy for sequence-level utility, and we report the silhouette
result mainly to document that negative finding.

\subsection{Which errors should target-domain adaptation prioritise?}
\label{sec:gap}

E3's pooled \mf\ falls from $0.7904$ in domain to $0.6099$ out of domain, a gap of $0.1805$. An
aggregate gap does not indicate whether adaptation requires broad retraining or a targeted correction.
We therefore localise the loss by class and confusion channel. This is a prioritisation analysis: it
identifies where an intervention could recover the most performance, but does not by itself identify
the physiological or acquisition mechanism that generated the errors. Throughout this analysis,
A$\rightarrow$B denotes a reference-labelled A epoch predicted as B; it does not denote a physiological
stage transition over time.

N3 is the dominant class loss: E3 N3 F1 falls from 0.8085 on Sleep-EDF to 0.4055 on SHHS1, with
precision 0.9440 but recall 0.2582. E0 shows nearly the same recall (0.2610) and N3$\rightarrow$N2
rate (72.3\% versus 73.1\% for E3), so E3's aggregate transfer advantage does not resolve N3 and the
failure cannot be assigned specifically to ResNet-1D or TCN. N2$\rightarrow$REM is the second-largest
E3 confusion channel.

The E3 oracle diagnostic ranks N3$\rightarrow$N2 and N2$\rightarrow$REM as potential adaptation targets,
corresponding to 74.5\% and 27.5\% of the numerical cross-cohort gap. These nonlinear values are neither
additive nor achievable-performance estimates. Reference-label boundary neighbourhoods are harder on
both cohorts, but low N3 recall in stable SHHS1 regions prevents a boundary-only explanation. These are
post-hoc offline diagnostics, not physiological-transition or causal analyses.

E6 provides a label-free but transductive sensitivity check. Its complete-target-record $z$-score lowers
N3 recall to 0.2005 and leaves boundary-neighbourhood recall essentially unchanged (0.0721 versus
0.0733 for E3). Thus, the tested normalisation is not a sufficient stand-alone remedy. Detailed
class-wise, confusion, oracle and transition results are in Supplementary Tables S7--S11.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

The first research question asked whether the sequence-model substitution and bundled E2 feature/context
replacement justified changing the pipeline. They did not establish a stable predictive advantage: both
in-domain configuration effects were small and non-significant after multiplicity correction, and the
bundled E2\,--\,E1 contrast reversed sign on SHHS1. The supported pipeline benefit is operational: the implemented ResNet-1D--TCN pipeline
reduced measured forward latency and observed training-and-validation wall-clock time, while increasing
parameter count and peak GPU memory. It did not establish superior predictive accuracy.

The second question asked which choices retained benefit across cohorts. The fixed-scale E3 procedure
outperformed its two locked references on SHHS1, and E3 ranked above E6 under both seeds. Under a fixed
ResNet-1D--TCN architecture, the post-hoc E3--E2 contrast was larger than the E1--E0 sequence-model
contrast and the bundled E2--E1 feature/context contrast;
the seed-123 E4 extension and the E5 identity audit associate band-pass filtering with most of this
difference and provide no evidence that fixed scaling adds a transfer benefit. These secondary contrasts
identify preprocessing as the more promising development axis, but they do not establish a causal or
universal contribution for any single operation.

The third question asked where adaptation should focus. N3$\rightarrow$N2 recurs at essentially the
same rate in E0 and E3, while the E3 oracle analysis ranks it above N2$\rightarrow$REM in potential
leverage; the N3 deficit becomes extreme near reference-label changes but remains substantial in stable
N3. The evidence therefore identifies a target-domain failure shared across the evaluated pipelines,
not an E3-specific weakness or a boundary-only phenomenon. Record-wise $z$-scoring does not repair N3,
so a labelled, class-specific target-domain adaptation experiment is better motivated than another
generic architecture or normalisation change.

\iffalse
\subsection{Interpretation of the supporting analyses}
\label{sec:rf}

The context-group and silhouette experiments are secondary checks rather than central contributions.
The context ablation tests the marginal value of supplying a $\pm1$-epoch summary at the encoder when
the downstream non-causal TCN already has a 253-epoch receptive field. Its null result therefore does
not test whether temporal context is useful in sleep staging; a direct test would need to vary the TCN
receptive field or input window. Likewise, the opposite rankings produced by silhouette score and
downstream performance show only that this particular geometric proxy is unsuitable for selecting the
encoder in this pipeline. Neither result changes the deployment conclusions drawn from the paired
cross-cohort comparisons.

\subsection{From error localisation to an adaptation decision}

The high precision and low recall of N3, the concentration of N3 errors in N2, and the further recall
loss near stage transitions are consistent with a conservative N3 decision boundary in SHHS1. E0's
near-identical N3 recall and N3$\rightarrow$N2 rate show that this failure persists across the two model
families evaluated; they do not identify a shared causal mechanism. Montage
and age are plausible contributors: the model was trained on Fpz--Cz in a younger cohort and evaluated
on C4--A1 in an older cohort, while slow-wave amplitude varies with both derivation and
age~\cite{massimini2004travelling,ohayon2004metaanalysis}. However, montage, population, acquisition and
scoring practice change together between the datasets. The present design cannot assign the failure to
one of them, and the precision--recall pattern alone does not identify an amplitude threshold as the
mechanism.

The difference in class prevalence is also relevant. A prior-shift correction would move N3 and N1 in
the required directions, but it was not fitted or validated here. It should therefore be evaluated as a
low-cost baseline in the next target-domain experiment, alongside class-specific calibration and
limited fine-tuning; the present error pattern does not justify dismissing it or treating it as a
complete solution.

E6 provides an important boundary on the interpretation. If removal of record-level absolute scale were
a sufficient correction, E6 should improve N3 recall over E3. Instead, E6 lowers whole-sample N3 recall
from 0.2582 to 0.2005 and leaves transition-region recall essentially unchanged (0.0721 versus 0.0733).
Thus, the data do not support absolute amplitude scale as a sufficient explanation, and record-wise
$z$-scoring is not a useful stand-alone adaptation strategy for this failure.

The actionable result is narrower but better supported. Before deployment on SHHS-like data, the next
experiment should target the N2--N3 decision boundary using labelled target-domain examples and should
report N3 recall specifically near transitions. A small calibration or fine-tuning study on the held-out
SHHS adaptation participants would test whether the boundary can be moved without increasing false N3
predictions. The N2--REM channel should be the second target. This prioritisation follows directly from
the error counts; the choice between montage-aware features, class-specific calibration and target-domain
fine-tuning remains an open experimental question.

\subsection{Architecture and preprocessing implications}

E1\,--\,E0 isolates the TCN substitution; E2\,--\,E1 jointly changes the encoder, explicit P/N context
and feature dimension and cannot establish a ResNet-only effect. Neither in-domain contrast survives
Holm correction, and the bundled E2\,--\,E1 contrast reverses on SHHS1. The reliable advantage of the
implemented ResNet-1D--TCN pipeline is therefore operational: a $3.76\times$ benchmark speed-up and
10.2--12.2$\times$ observed training-and-validation speed-up, paid for with more parameters and memory.
In the observed post-hoc E3\,--\,E2 comparison, preprocessing is the stronger development axis. The E4
extension associates band-pass filtering with much of that difference, but the opened cohort and two
fixed seeds preclude a causal or universal claim about any operation.

\subsection{Positioning relative to published results}

We do not claim state-of-the-art benchmark performance. The wake-trimming
policy alone (Section~3.1) moves the wake fraction, and hence accuracy and macro-F1, by an amount
comparable to every architectural effect measured here; subject-count, split scheme, and channel choice
compound this. Published Sleep-EDF figures for the models discussed in
Section~2~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet,eldele2021attnsleep,phan2022sleeptransformer,
jadhav2024zleepanlystnet} were obtained under protocols that differ in several of these dimensions
simultaneously, so a numerical comparison would misrepresent the evidence in either direction. Our E0
control is a re-implementation of the ZleepAnlystNet design evaluated under \emph{our} protocol, and it
is the reference for the component questions posed here. The study should therefore be read as evidence
about relative design choices and transfer failure under a shared protocol, not as a leaderboard claim.

\subsection{Limitations and a prioritised agenda}

Results should be read within the data and protocol evaluated. SHHS1 is one specific out-of-domain
sample, not a population. The second seed corroborates direction but two fixed seeds cannot characterise
initialisation variance; a proper treatment would require enough seeds to estimate a distribution, and
we report no such claim. Several component and reference-transition analyses were run after the sample
was opened and are supporting evidence only. Single-channel EEG limits separability for N1 and REM in
principle, and the transition neighbourhood uses reference labels on both sides of a boundary, so it is
an offline benchmarking device rather than a deployable rule. No probability-calibration or selective-
prediction analysis was performed, which matters for clinical use where an abstain option would be
valuable~\cite{guo2017calibration,phan2022sleeptransformer}.

Two reporting gaps remain. Absolute-performance intervals are available for the SHHS
configurations (Table~\ref{tab:shhs-performance}) but not for the Sleep-EDF ones, where only
difference intervals were retained. And although paired win/tie/loss counts and median subject-level
differences were preserved for every comparison, the full per-subject \mf\ distributions were not
archived, so we cannot report the dispersion (IQR, range) of individual performance or identify which
participants the models handle worst. For a metric whose clinical relevance is inherently per-patient
this is a limitation. Per-class SHHS metrics have now been recovered for E6 from the locked prediction
artifacts. Pooled class-wise results for E0 have also been recovered from the locked run manifest and
support the cross-model N3 comparison; transition-neighbourhood and stable-interior metrics are available
for E0 and E3 on both cohorts. Corresponding class-wise results for E1 remain unavailable.

Ordered by expected practical value, we would prioritise: (1) limited class-specific fine-tuning or
decision-boundary adjustment on the held-out SHHS adaptation participants, evaluated on N3 recall and
false-positive rate in both boundary and stable regions; (2) fitting and evaluating calibration/selective
prediction on independent target validation data; and (3) estimating initialisation variance with a
sufficient number of seeds. The first two address the deployment failure identified here; the third
improves the strength of the methodological evidence.

\section{Conclusion}

A shared subject-wise protocol allowed six active configurations to be compared on the same Sleep-EDF
folds; a seventh planned configuration, E5, was excluded before performance analysis because it was
bitwise identical to E4.
The isolated TCN substitution and bundled E2 feature/context replacement did not establish a stable
predictive advantage after correction for multiple comparisons. ResNet-1D--TCN nevertheless offers an operational alternative that is
$3.76\times$ faster in the controlled forward benchmark and 10.2--12.2$\times$ faster in observed
training-and-validation wall-clock time, at the cost of more parameters and memory.

On SHHS1, the fixed-scale E3 procedure outperformed both locked references, while the largest observed
component difference was associated with preprocessing under a fixed architecture. This result shifts
the practical priority from further architecture replacement to signal handling and target-domain
evaluation, without establishing any individual preprocessing operation as causal.

Most importantly, the aggregate transfer loss resolves into concrete adaptation targets. E0 and E3
both systematically under-call N3 as N2, showing that E3's overall transfer advantage does not solve
N3 detection; N2 is also frequently over-called as REM by E3. Per-record $z$-scoring does not repair
the N3 deficit, and the cross-cohort diagnostic shows that reference-label boundaries intensify errors
without fully explaining the stable-region SHHS1 deficit. Thus, neither the evaluated architecture changes
nor generic normalisation is a sufficient deployment remedy. The evidence supports a targeted, labelled adaptation study on these decision
boundaries; it does not yet support an architecture-specific, physiological or clinical claim.

\subsection*{Reproducibility and data availability}

Source code, experimental protocols, data partitions, run manifests and aggregate analysis outputs are
retained in the project repository. Sleep-EDF Expanded is publicly available from PhysioNet. SHHS is
accessed through the NSRR under its data use agreement; participant-level derived data are not
redistributed. Checkpoints are verified by SHA-256 before loading, and the locked SHHS predictions used
for the reported analyses were hash-verified against their run manifests. The E6 class-wise results are
therefore reported as a descriptive reanalysis of these locked predictions. The historical locked-protocol
snapshot \texttt{configs/shhs\_zero\_shot\_v1.json} is retained in the repository and its SHA-256 matches the
protocol hash recorded in the SHHS run manifest (`165d7cdf...fe93`). The newer
\texttt{configs/shhs\_v1\_protocol.json} has a different SHA-256 (`9541e233...fe9`) because it is an expanded
post-run audit record; it must not be substituted for the historical snapshot when interpreting the locked
predictions. Protocol provenance is therefore traceable, although the locked E6 results remain a
descriptive reanalysis rather than a rerun.
